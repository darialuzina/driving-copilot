# Introduction


This Driving Procedure describes the most desirable driving behaviour for drivers of a passenger car (category B). In order to be authorised to drive on public roads, candidates sitting the category B practical test must also demonstrate this behaviour within the specified standards.
The content of this Driving Procedure is based on current road traffic legislation (Road Traffic Act 1994, the 1990 Regulations on Traffic Rules and Road Signs and the Vehicles Regulations), on the applicable examination requirements and on the learning objectives document for Category B driving licences, as drawn up on behalf of the Ministry of Transport, Public Works and Water Management. Desirable behaviour on the road consists, to a large extent, of the correct application of the traffic rules. In cases where the traffic regulations are limited to general rules, the correct execution of the various driving manoeuvres is described in accordance with the statutory driving test requirements.
In addition, the Driving Test Procedure places considerable emphasis on defensive and considerate driving behaviour. Taking the interests of other road users into account plays a key role in this. The importance of environmentally conscious driving behaviour is also recognised. The high accident rate among novice drivers has prompted the Minister for Infrastructure and the Environment to require novice drivers to be better prepared for everyday driving situations. The GDE matrix refers to these as ‘higher-order skills’. By focusing, during driving instruction, on driving independently with a sat-nav and on self-reflection, candidates are better equipped to respond to the demands placed on them by modern traffic conditions. As the Driving Procedure is primarily intended for the driving instructor and the examiner, the authors have chosen to describe driving behaviour on the basis of the examination components and the assessment criteria used in the CBR practical test.
Chapter 1 covers preparing the vehicle for the road, its operation and control, and environmentally conscious driving behaviour. Chapter 2 describes the desirable, appropriate, decisive and considerate driving behaviour required for safe participation in road traffic. Chapter 3 covers only those assessment topics that are relevant to the specific examination components in question. An overview of all components and assessment topics is included in Appendix 1 of this Driving Test Procedure. The topics in Chapters 1 and 2 are of a general nature and apply to all examination components described in Chapter 3 (Chapters 1 and 2 serve as an ‘overarching framework’). The examiner therefore takes into account the assessment topics from Chapters 1 and 2 for each component of road user behaviour. The traffic rules and road signs are not described or explained in the Driving Procedure. Users of this Driving Procedure are required to have a full command of road traffic theory. It is expressly pointed out that this Driving Procedure does not contain any behavioural analyses, nor is it a lesson plan. The instructor must regard the content of the Driving Procedure as the learning objective for their pupils. Thanks to the detailed description of the desired driving behaviour, the instructor knows what is expected of their learner during the test. The method of instruction is, of course, entirely at the instructor’s discretion. The examiner assesses the candidate’s driving behaviour on the basis of the Driving Procedure. During the practical test, the examiner determines whether, and if so to what extent, the behaviour displayed deviates from that set out in the Driving Procedure. Depending on the nature and severity of the non-compliant behaviour and the number of times it occurs, he will either declare the candidate competent to drive or not.
The Driving Procedure can also be useful to people other than instructors and examiners. For driving licence holders, for example, the Driving Procedure serves as a comprehensive reference guide that enables them to critically assess their own driving skills. Vehicles are equipped with driver assistance systems. These are also known as ADAS (Advanced Driver Assistance Systems). An overview of common driver assistance systems can be found in the ‘ADAS Dictionary’ published by the ADAS Alliance.
To ensure the most appropriate use of these driver assistance systems, general guidelines have been included in Chapters 1 and 2 of this driving procedure in 2023. These guidelines have been developed on the basis of the following principles:
1. The driver is always personally responsible for carrying out the task of driving; this responsibility does not lie with the car or the driver assistance systems. 2. Good driving ability is demonstrated by safe, comfortable, considerate and appropriate driving. This may be achieved with or without driver assistance systems. 3. The driver has a reasonable understanding of the purpose of driver assistance systems, how they work and how to use them to the best of their ability. 4. The driver is aware that: a. the names of driver assistance systems may vary between cars (models and makes); b. although driver assistance systems have the same functionality, their design or method of operation may differ between cars (models and makes); c. road, weather and traffic conditions may affect the operation of driver assistance systems. 5. The driver knows what a driver assistance system is capable of and that it has limitations. 6. The driver can consciously allow the driver assistance systems to provide information or support (with the exception of emergency systems). 7. The driver recognises the risk of their own behaviour changing for the worse as a result of the driver assistance system. For example, maintaining a shorter following distance because the car will brake automatically in emergency situations anyway.

# 1. Preparing the vehicle for the road, operation/control and environmentally conscious driving


Chapter 1 of these Driving Procedures applies to all the components described in Chapter 3.
Vehicle technology is advancing rapidly. Chapter 1 describes how to use common controls. For example, it explains how to operate the brakes or start the engine.


## Preparing the vehicle for the road and operation/control




## Preparing the car for the road


Before the vehicle is driven on public roads, it must be made roadworthy. This involves adjusting the seat, mirrors and steering wheel as described in this section. The seat belt fitted must be used in the prescribed manner.


### Seating and handlebar position


The driver must be able to sit comfortably behind the wheel. They must also be able to carry out all the necessary actions without having to move around unnecessarily.
In most cars, the steering wheel can be adjusted for height and distance from the driver. This ensures the driver is in the optimal position relative to the steering wheel, in combination with the adjustment of the seat cushion and backrest. The steering wheel and/or seat should not be adjusted whilst driving. When seated correctly, you have sufficient support and can feel what is happening to the car. A correctly adjusted headrest provides maximum protection for the neck and head in the event of a collision, particularly in a rear-end collision. Therefore, any headrest fitted must be correctly adjusted to suit the height of the driver and any passengers. If the headrest can be tilted, the gap between the head and the headrest should be kept as small as possible.
A good seating position is achieved when the seat and headrest are optimally adjusted to suit the driver. This is the case if:
− rest your thighs on the seat for as long as possible
− with the back fully pressed against the backrest, the top of the steering wheel can still just about be gripped with both hands
− the left leg is still slightly bent when the clutch pedal is fully depressed. Even in a car with an automatic gearbox, the left leg is slightly bent
− there is a clear view of the road
− the top of the headrest is level with the top of the head.


### Steering control


We distinguish between the pass-through method and the over-the-top method. The pass-through method is preferable. However, the over-the-top method can be used effectively when steering quickly at low speeds.
When turning the steering wheel back, guide it with both hands until the wheels are straight again. When reversing, you may turn your upper body so that your right hand is no longer holding the steering wheel.
A good riding posture is achieved when
− the steering wheel is not gripped tightly with both hands
− the hands are positioned at approximately ‘a quarter to three’
− the arms are slightly bent
− the hands are positioned on the outside of the steering wheel
Comments
− Avoid resting your left arm on the door. This can restrict your range of movement in relation to the handlebars
− keep both hands on the steering wheel, unless you are operating the gear lever, handbrake or controls
− Avoid turning the handlebars with your hands crossed over each other. This restricts your ability to steer.


### Adjusting the mirrors


The mirrors are correctly adjusted when they provide the best possible view.
The rear-view mirror is correctly adjusted if:
− the vertical centre of the rear window is visible in the vertical centre of the mirror
− the height is adjusted to ensure optimum visibility.
The wing mirrors are correctly adjusted if:
− the (possibly imaginary) horizon is visible at about a quarter of the way down the top of those mirrors
− the left-hand side of the car is still just visible in the left-hand wing mirror, and as much of the section of road to the left of and behind the car as possible can be seen
− the right-hand side of the car is just visible in the right-hand wing mirror and as much of the road to the right of and behind the car as possible can be seen.
Comments
− The mirrors must be adjusted before setting off
− when adjusting the unit, contact with the glass is avoided as far as possible
− where ‘mirror’ is stated or implied, this may also be read as ‘camera-monitor system’


### Wear a seatbelt


The seat belts fitted must be used in the prescribed manner.
This means that they should be worn ‘tightly’ against the body to ensure optimum safety. This applies in particular to the lower part (hip) of the seatbelt. Devices designed to minimise discomfort caused by the seatbelt (such as clips, etc.) are strongly discouraged for safety reasons.


## Operation/control


Proficiency in handling the vehicle is the basis for complete control of it in all circumstances.
A large number of technical operations will need to be carried out almost automatically. It goes without saying that the driver must be able to switch on and off, independently, equipment such as the windscreen and side window demisters, the rear window heater, the windscreen wipers and the lights.
This must not cause the driver’s attention to be diverted from the road or jeopardise their control of the vehicle. Familiarity with the location and operation of the various controls and switches – including those for driver assistance systems – is therefore absolutely essential.


### Starting the engine


− the parking brake must be engaged
− Move the gear lever to neutral. For a car with an automatic gearbox, move the gear lever to park
− Do not switch on any electrical appliances yet
− Press the clutch pedal all the way down. In a car with an automatic gearbox, press the brake pedal
− Establish contact without starting the engine. Check that all the relevant indicator lights are on (light test)
− Start the engine in accordance with the operating instructions
− checking the dashboard (indicator lights, gauges, etc.)
− check brake pressure (press the brake pedal)
− whilst the engine is running, release the clutch pedal slowly, unless you are going to change gear and drive off immediately afterwards.


### Switch off the engine


− apply the parking brake
− Move the gear lever to neutral. For a car with an automatic gearbox, move the gear lever to park.
− switch off any unnecessary electrical appliances
− switch off the engine
− release the clutch pedal.
If you anticipate a ‘longer’ traffic hold (lasting longer than 1 minute), switch off the engine to help protect the environment. If the vehicle is fitted with a start-stop system, this system will automatically switch the engine off and on again.
When leaving the car, also remove and take your key, keycard or similar with you. Then lock the car.


### Clutch, gear-change mechanism and ‘throttle’ control


1 The clutch, gearshift mechanism and throttle must be operated correctly and safely.


### Coupling


The clutch engages (or disengages) the connection between the engine and the driven wheels. It must be operated with care to minimise wear and ensure that engagement and disengagement are smooth.
Operation The clutch pedal is operated using the ball of the left foot and must always be fully depressed. The foot should only be in contact with the pedal whilst operating it. When the clutch pedal is not being operated, the foot must not rest on or above the pedal. Preferably, rest your foot to the left of the clutch pedal. Only when pulling away, driving slowly or reversing very slowly may the speed be controlled using the clutch. When braking, disengage the clutch just before the engine reaches idle speed.
In a car with an automatic gearbox, the car controls the engagement and disengagement of the clutch.


### Gear mechanism


Proper and timely gear changes (both when accelerating and decelerating) ensure optimum power transfer from the engine to the driven wheels.
In a car with an automatic gearbox, the car controls the gear-changing mechanism.
Hold the gear lever loosely. The minimum and maximum engine revs must not be exceeded in any gear. This is in accordance with the relevant car’s owner’s manual. Whilst the engine is cold, the revs are limited. Environmental considerations also play a part here.
Comments:
− do not operate the gear lever too forcefully
− avoid unnecessary gear changes
− Driving too slowly in a gear that is too high can cause engine damage, as can leaving the engine in one gear for too long
− Selecting the correct gear is important in order to make the most of the engine’s pulling power.
Engaging the clutch when setting off: Press the clutch pedal all the way down. Gently move the gear lever into first or reverse gear.
1 In the driving instructions, terms such as ‘acceleration’ and ‘pressing the accelerator’ are used for clarity. This applies to various types of transmission and fuels.
In a car with an automatic gearbox, you must press the brake pedal and move the gear lever into the forward or reverse position.
How to drive off correctly: Release the clutch whilst simultaneously increasing the throttle. Once the clutch engages, hold both the clutch and accelerator pedals steady for a moment. If the handbrake is engaged, release it. Continue to release the clutch pedal whilst simultaneously increasing the throttle (engine speed).
A car with an automatic gearbox starts moving when the brake pedal is released. In some cases, it may be necessary to increase the throttle at this point.
Changing up: In a single movement, fully depress the clutch pedal whilst simultaneously reducing the throttle; gently move the gear lever through neutral into the next gear, engage the clutch and increase the throttle at the same time. Note: Avoid jerky movements when changing gear.
Downshifting: Whilst reducing the throttle, fully depress the clutch pedal in a single movement; gently shift the gear lever through neutral into the desired gear, release the clutch and adjust the throttle. When stopping in traffic, shift into first gear immediately after coming to a standstill; the clutch pedal will naturally remain depressed. For a longer stop: move the gear lever to neutral and release the clutch pedal. Apply the handbrake if necessary. Cars fitted with the ‘stop/start system’ will then switch off the engine. As soon as the clutch pedal is depressed again, the engine will restart.


### Gas supply


The throttle (indirectly) controls the amount of fuel supplied to the engine and, consequently, the engine speed. Together with factors such as the selected gear, this determines the engine’s speed and pulling power.
The accelerator pedal is operated with the right foot. Keeping the heel as flat on the floor as possible helps to ensure the correct amount of throttle is applied. You must avoid the throttle becoming ‘jerky’ or the engine ‘revving out of control’.


#### Operating the brakes


The brakes fitted must be used correctly and in good time.
The car can be braked in the following ways:
− by reducing the gas supply
− by using the brakes.
Technical mastery of the brakes is demonstrated through their deliberate modulation and use. Whilst operating the brakes, it is permissible to change down a gear, whilst ensuring that the clutch is not used to slow the vehicle down sharply. When braking is required: as a general rule, do not change down a gear. This is an unnecessary manoeuvre which, moreover, can cause additional wear and tear to the vehicle. When braking, prevent the wheels from locking. For longer stops, apply the handbrake if necessary.


### Braking by reducing the fuel supply


Operation:
− operate the accelerator pedal in a timely and effective manner to slow the vehicle down smoothly. This also applies when using regenerative braking.


### Braking using the service brake


Operation:
− the brake pedal is operated with the ball of the right foot
− distribute braking as evenly as possible over the available distance
− if braking continues until the car comes to a standstill, the braking pressure just before it comes to a standstill
reduce it to such an extent that the ‘final shock’ is avoided
− In principle, braking is carried out in the coupled state right up until the vehicle comes to a standstill.


### Handbrake


− is operated by hand or foot
− is only used when the vehicle is stationary.


#### General comments on vehicle operation


If a different gear is selected for a bend, you must have changed gear and disengaged the clutch before entering the bend. Drive smoothly through the second half of the bend with the engine running at a ‘light load’. Avoid braking whilst cornering as much as possible.
If you need to accelerate properly (for example, when merging), press the accelerator harder and change up to a higher gear later.
If circumstances permit, you may wish to ease off the accelerator earlier when merging, in the interests of fuel-efficient driving. Avoid changing back to a lower gear when back on the main carriageway.


#### Control of the lighting and equipment


Where lighting and/or electrical equipment is to be used, the following applies:
− this (on one’s own initiative) is done correctly
− its operation does not detract from the driver’s attention to the traffic and control of the vehicle
− this does not take any longer than is necessary or desirable.
This section covers how to use the correct lighting and the (auxiliary) equipment fitted in the car, such as:
− windscreen and side window demisting
− horn
− windscreen wipers
− rear window heater
− interior heating
− air conditioning
− sat-nav
− driver assistance systems.
It is essential to be familiar with the interior, the layout of the dashboard and the functions of the various controls.


### Driving at night and in conditions that severely impair visibility.


During the period specified by law and in other relevant circumstances, appropriate lighting must be provided.
In principle, dipped headlights should be used. Full-beam headlights may only be used at night and may be advisable, for example, when visibility is limited in relation to the speed at which the vehicle is travelling. When using full-beam headlights, care must be taken not to cause a nuisance to others.
Circumstances that may seriously impair visibility in this context include:
− fog
− snowfall
− rain
− glaring sunlight
− insufficient daylight in tunnels
− insufficient daylight due to shade cast by trees
− driving in multi-storey car parks.
When most drivers use their lights, it is safe to assume that this is indeed necessary. It is important to note that lights are not only used to see better, but above all to be seen more clearly.
Comments
− extra care is needed around stationary and slow-moving vehicles
− When approaching oncoming vehicles with their headlights on, it is better to look to the side rather than directly into the lights; this prevents glare
− Roadside posts and kerb lines can be a useful means of orientation.


#### Vehicle control


This topic concerns overall control of the vehicle. This is the case when the driver maintains full and independent control of the vehicle whilst carrying out the various manoeuvres.
These actions involve the technical operation of the vehicle in conjunction with the required visual awareness and steering control. Recognising a traffic or road situation in good time and reacting to it, combined with correct vehicle control, enhances the driver’s control of the vehicle. Under normal traffic conditions, the driver operates the car correctly from a technical point of view and is in control of the car.


### Characteristics and condition of the vehicle


To maintain good control of a vehicle, it is essential to be familiar with its characteristics. The position of the car’s centre of gravity has a significant influence on its directional stability, steering characteristics and handling when cornering. It is useful to know which of the car’s wheels are driven. The condition of the vehicle can also influence its road holding. Examples of this include the presence of passengers and cargo, and the way the vehicle is loaded. It is also important to be fully aware of the vehicle’s dimensions.


### Risk of slipping


In situations where there is an increased risk of skidding, the way the car is driven must be adapted accordingly. Care must be taken to prevent the car from skidding.
Skidding occurs when the frictional resistance between the tyre and the road surface is exceeded. This can be caused by:
− braking too hard/abruptly
− road camber, gradients, crosswinds
− releasing the clutch too quickly
− accelerating too quickly
− steering too abruptly
− taking a bend too fast
− the type and condition of the tyres. The type and condition of the road surface, or any (sudden) change in these, may also contribute to a skid occurring.
This driving procedure does not cover how to correct a skid.


### Steering stability


The vehicle must maintain a steady course at all times. When operating the technical controls and auxiliary equipment, the vehicle must always be kept on the correct course.
Whilst complying with the legal requirements regarding the sections of road to be followed, the aim is to drive ‘the ideal line’ with as few steering corrections as possible. Unnecessary and abrupt steering movements should be avoided. The driver’s gaze, other behaviours and/or the performance of various actions must not adversely affect the control of the vehicle.


## Environmentally conscious driving


Environmentally conscious driving applies to each of the seven parts of the test and therefore serves as an overarching principle. The driver makes their own choices, so it is possible to assess these. The driver knows how to drive in an environmentally conscious manner and puts this into practice.


### Preparing for a ride


It is important that a journey is planned in such a way as to minimise its impact on the environment. The time and route must therefore be chosen to strike a good balance between the distance to be travelled and the expected traffic flow. Avoiding times and road sections prone to congestion is fundamental to this. Investigating whether alternative transport options are available and viable, or whether the journey can be combined with other travellers, can also reduce the environmental impact. Driving with unnecessary loads is undesirable. This includes carrying unnecessary ballast, as well as driving with accessories that increase aerodynamic drag, such as ski boxes, roof racks, etc.


### Vehicle conditions


The technical condition of a car is important in terms of the level of (harmful) emissions. Delaying routine maintenance can have a negative effect on the efficiency of fuel combustion. This is one of the reasons why it is important to have (preventative) maintenance carried out by a specialist company in accordance with the manufacturer’s specifications. Tyre pressure is important. Checking your tyres regularly and ensuring they are at the correct pressure is part of good journey preparation. This check and/or adjustment should be carried out every month.


### Vehicle controls


The use of unnecessary power-consuming devices should be avoided. In particular, energy-consuming devices such as air conditioning and electric windscreen heating should not be left on unnecessarily (for long periods). Modern vehicle technology includes an engine management system that ensures the most efficient possible use of fuel. However, in a manual car, the choice of gear is left to the driver. The driver can therefore influence fuel consumption. Driving in a relatively high gear reduces fuel consumption. Provided it does not compromise safety in the given traffic conditions and does not have an unacceptable effect on traffic flow, gear changes should be made between 2,000 and 2,500 revolutions per minute.
When accelerating, provided the following distance allows, the accelerator should be pressed firmly. This involves pressing the accelerator pedal down to at least half its available travel. The load on the clutch during the slip phase must not be too high. It is important to make use of the vehicle’s rolling momentum. Releasing the accelerator pedal too early, as soon as it becomes apparent that deceleration is required, ensures that the fuel and air intake is cut off. It is important that the engine remains engaged in the gear currently being driven in. When coming to a stop, the driver should depress the clutch pedal just before the engine reaches idle speed.
Leaving the engine idling wastes fuel and therefore causes unnecessary emissions of harmful substances. Furthermore, this often causes unnecessary noise pollution for the surrounding area. When stopping for a period expected to last longer than one minute, it is advisable to switch off the engine. It is important to ensure that safety is not compromised and that, if necessary, you can drive off again immediately. Ensure that the mandatory lights remain on and, if necessary, the windscreen heater is kept running. Correct use of cruise control helps maintain a constant speed, thereby reducing fuel consumption. Where available and appropriate, the use of cruise control is recommended.


### Anticipatory driving


Driving at a constant speed as much as possible reduces fuel consumption. If a change in traffic conditions is spotted in good time, it is possible to respond to it promptly. It is also very important to take into account situations that may develop. To do this, it is essential to look far ahead and remain alert. Maintaining a safe following distance is a basic requirement.


## Driver assistance systems


When using advanced driver-assistance systems (ADAS), the following guidelines apply to ‘preparing the vehicle for driving, operation/control and environmentally conscious driving behaviour’:
− Before setting off, the driver checks which driver-assistance systems in the car directly affect driving and how they work.
− The driver checks that the sensors and cameras are sufficiently clean before setting off. He remains vigilant to ensure that the driver assistance systems are functioning as effectively as possible. The driver responds appropriately to any anomalies.
− The driver uses the settings of driver assistance systems in a way that promotes the most appropriate driving behaviour in accordance with this driving procedure.
− Even when using driver assistance systems, the driver maintains an active driving posture, enabling them to take control of the vehicle at a moment’s notice. They also keep a constant eye on the traffic situation and the driver assistance systems. The driver therefore does not allow themselves to be distracted from their driving task and responsibility.


# 2. Taking part in road traffic correctly and safely


This chapter describes the most desirable driving behaviour in general terms. Reference is made to Article 5 of the 1994 Road Traffic Act and to socially accepted standards for considerate and defensive driving.
Chapter 2 of these Driving Procedures applies to all the components described in Chapter 3.


## Traffic Insights


In order to take part in traffic correctly and safely, ‘traffic awareness’ is one of the things required.
This means identifying and responding to the following in good time:
− specific traffic situations
− situations that are set to develop
− situations that may develop in a certain way.
The following factors play a role in this:
− human beings
− the road and its surroundings
− the vehicle
− the weather conditions.
One should take into account circumstances that require particular caution when approaching and/or passing, such as:
− poor, crumbling road verges, particularly those adjacent to lower sections of the road
− objects lying on the carriageway that may pose a danger to traffic
− objects that are too close to the carriageway
− branches, signboards, awnings, etc. protruding over the carriageway
− narrow passages at roadworks, bridges, entrances and exits, slip roads, etc.
− sections of road with temporary traffic signs and/or road markings
− the vehicle skidding (veering off course) when driving through a deep puddle
− gusts of wind
− rutting and other longitudinal irregularities
− materials lying on the carriageway, such as sand, gravel, chippings, leaves and oil
− milled sections of road
− wildlife grids
− loose and/or protruding loads on vehicles
− bumpy and poorly maintained sections of road with cracks and/or potholes
− asphalt melting due to very hot weather.
The speed must be adjusted so that the vehicle stops as little as possible.
− Examples of situations where good anticipation prevents the need to stop include:
− turn left or right if there is a single road user on an adjacent cycle path, cycle/moped path or footpath (it is better to ride diagonally behind a cyclist or moped rider travelling at the same speed than to overtake them first and then stop in front of them)
− overtaking a vehicle stationary on the right-hand side of a two-lane carriageway, whilst approaching a single oncoming vehicle
− approaching a stationary traffic jam (rear-ending it), which has almost started moving again
− a junction where only a single other vehicle may be present at the same time or almost at the same time.
Notes: As the driver of the lead vehicle in a convoy of vehicles travelling one behind the other, try to keep moving as much as possible. This is safer and also helps to improve the flow of traffic. It also saves fuel and is more environmentally friendly.
If stopping is unavoidable, then – provided this improves safety – warn the traffic behind by flashing the brake lights or hazard warning lights.
If stopping is unavoidable, care must be taken to ensure that oncoming and cross traffic is not obstructed:
− on the left-hand half of the carriageway, intended for oncoming traffic
− at a level crossing
− at a pedestrian crossing or a crossing for cyclists and moped riders
− at a junction
− on a marked section of road leading to or from a junction.
One must be sufficiently alert to ensure that changes expected in the traffic situation do not come as such a surprise that one is forced to brake and/or steer abnormally. Changes which, in principle, should not come as a surprise include, for example:
− a bus or tram stopping at a bus stop or tram stop
− a passenger crossing the road near a stationary bus or tram
− the door of a vehicle that has just stopped (at the kerb) opening outwards
− a car driving away which the driver had just got into (look out for exhaust fumes, a reversing light that’s on, etc.)
− a car driving off after a passenger has got into it
− a driver who swerves to avoid an object on the carriageway (or puddles/potholes)
− a vehicle ahead that brakes in response to an event that demands attention (flashing lights on special vehicles, aircraft flying overhead, accidents, etc.).
You should always be aware of the mistakes that other road users might make, which frequently lead to accidents. For example:
− cutting corners
− failing to keep a safe distance from the vehicle in front
− approaching and crossing junctions at excessive speed
− braking too hard/too late
− driving at an inappropriate speed
− overtaking in places and at times when it is not safe to do so
− changing lanes suddenly, etc.
We should avoid getting ‘stuck’ as much as possible. For example:
− having to stop behind a vehicle that is ‘double-parked’, because the situation was not recognised in time and overtaking drivers prevented them from getting past
− being unable to move back to the left into the pre-sorted position, due to a delayed response to a ‘wave’ of inhalers
− having to slow down on the motorway behind a slow-moving lorry because the overtaking manoeuvre was started too late.
Comments
− Don’t set off on a car journey when you’re tired or irritable
− take the forecast weather into account
− Avoid rushing and allow plenty of time to prepare for a long car journey
− use a sat-nav or consult a road map in advance when driving in or to an unfamiliar area
− During a storm, be aware of falling pieces of wood and similar objects
− Do not allow yourself to be distracted whilst driving by passengers, the car radio and/or the use of a mobile phone
− use mirrored windows to see further down a street
− cross a road, if possible with ‘cover’ from a heavy vehicle, into the flow of traffic
− try to avoid dangerous situations on the road as much as possible (e.g. do not enter a ‘funnel’ alongside a long articulated vehicle, etc.)
− try as far as possible to assess the overall road and traffic conditions
− divide your attention
− look in order to observe and be able to respond to what is seen
− where possible, ensure there is an ‘escape route’
− respond to the ‘language of the road’
− help to ensure that everyone works together as effectively as possible on the roads.
In addition to an understanding of traffic, the topics covered in this chapter are essential for safe and responsible participation in road traffic. These topics are of a general nature and relate to participation in road traffic as a whole. They concern aspects of driving ability that are regarded as ‘basic behaviour’. Every driver should have a sufficient command of this behaviour, regardless of the manoeuvre being performed or the situation in which they find themselves.


## Appropriate and decisive behaviour


This topic concerns the speed at which a vehicle is driven and the smoothness with which driving manoeuvres are carried out in relation to other traffic and/or the prevailing road conditions.
This topic also covers the decisiveness required in traffic, which is essential for carrying out actions and reacting to traffic situations.
Appropriate behaviour: Subject to the applicable regulations, drivers must always drive at a speed appropriate to the other traffic and the overall traffic and road conditions. A driver who adapts their driving sufficiently to the flow of traffic does not unnecessarily obstruct other road users and ensures that they can continue their journey as smoothly as possible. This means not driving unnecessarily slowly to the extent that other road users might be held up.
Without prejudice to observing the speed limit and being able to bring the motor vehicle to a halt in good time, the vehicle must always be driven at a speed that does not, and cannot, cause any danger or unnecessary nuisance to other road users. If the situation so requires, the driver must slow down or stop in good time. This slowing down or stopping must be carried out in a manner appropriate to the situation.
By adapting one’s driving style, all necessary manoeuvres can be carried out in a controlled manner. This also has a positive effect on the overall traffic situation, resulting in smooth and safe traffic flow.
In order to adapt their behaviour, the driver must:
− allows himself time and space to adapt his behaviour to the situation
− does not trust other road users to always behave correctly
− constantly asks themselves: “Can other road users see me? Have they seen me? If they haven’t seen me, what is my escape route?”
Decisive behaviour: The behaviour must demonstrate a certain degree of decisiveness.
Hesitant behaviour on the road must be avoided, except in situations that can be described as ‘unusual’ or ‘unexpected’. Unusual and uncertain behaviour will cause other road users to doubt how a developing traffic situation will unfold. A manoeuvre that has been planned and already begun should therefore, in principle, be carried out once the preparatory actions have been performed correctly.
Under normal circumstances, one should avoid giving way unnecessarily, allowing others to pass unnecessarily, or waiting longer than is necessary given the situation. Nevertheless, in order to improve traffic flow or promote safer road conditions, it is sometimes advisable to act in a manner other than that prescribed. In such cases, eye contact or hand signals between the parties involved can facilitate communication and clarify any intentions.


## The interests of other road users


This topic examines defensive and social behaviour in conjunction with road awareness.
Defensive behaviour means preventing accidents as far as possible, not just by
to make as few mistakes as possible, whilst also taking into account potential mistakes made by others and adjusting their driving style in good time to changing road and/or traffic conditions. The driver takes vulnerable road users into account and mitigates the mistakes of others as effectively as possible.
Defensive driving is based primarily on:
− insight
− vigilance
− anticipate (foresee possible developments)
− looking effectively (looking in order to really take things in)
− reply.
Considerate driving means being willing to help other road users in situations where this is necessary or desirable, even if those road users have made a mistake – whether consciously or unconsciously.
Considerate driving is characterised by:
− respecting the interests of other road users
− being aware of one’s own individual responsibility for road safety.
Examples of considerate driving include:
− leaving space for overtaking drivers in the event of a narrowing of the carriageway (zipping)
− giving way to, and allowing as unimpeded a passage as possible for, vehicles that are clearly part of a funeral procession
− avoiding driving through puddles if this causes a nuisance to others
− to enable a driver to exit a slip road.
The aim must always be to promote the safest and most favourable traffic conditions possible.
You should behave in such a way that other road users are not taken by surprise. For example:
− do not turn sharply to the left behind a parked vehicle in the direction of an oncoming driver
− only use the horn or give a light signal when necessary
− Do not brake or stop unnecessarily or abruptly.
You should avoid causing annoyance to other road users. For example:
− do not try to overtake at the last minute before a red traffic light, a closed level crossing or bridge, a stationary traffic jam, etc.
− in the event of a traffic jam or other traffic congestion, do not deliberately increase your speed or block off a space that another driver might have wished to use
Efforts are constantly being made to avert imminent danger by:
− to reduce speed
− to swerve
− to stop
− to increase speed.
If it is likely that the danger cannot be averted by one of these methods, a sound or light signal should be given, depending on the situation.
For the benefit of other drivers (approaching from behind), the flashing warning lights are activated if certain traffic and/or weather conditions require the driver to slow down suddenly or stop.
This applies in particular to:
− when traffic jams form
− when stopping in places where visibility is poor
− when the vehicle comes to a halt and visibility is insufficient, due to darkness or weather conditions, to notice the vehicle in good time.
Note: Using flashing warning lights does not make a traffic offence legal!
In particular, the interests of more vulnerable road users must be taken into account. Children, the elderly and people with disabilities are especially vulnerable in traffic:
− Children’s behaviour in traffic is generally unpredictable
− the reaction times of elderly and disabled people, and their ability to act quickly, are often reduced
− People with hearing impairments can be difficult to recognise as such
− blind people can only rely on their hearing
− account is taken of the playful manner in which young cyclists, moped riders and the like may take part in traffic. By recognising their presence and any unusual riding behaviour in good time, it is possible to respond appropriately
− Pedestrians are at greater risk when they are on the carriageway. They must be spotted in good time so that the appropriate action can be taken
− Care must be taken to anticipate larger groups of pedestrians, such as processions, columns and the like. The overall traffic situation must be assessed correctly when approaching these groups.
Furthermore, special consideration is given to, for example, people leading draught animals, pack animals and livestock, who are also more vulnerable. The same applies to cyclists who are accompanying small children on their bikes or transporting tools and the like.
Comments: Dangerous situations on the roads are often caused by human error. Don’t blame others for their mistakes, but help one another.
If another road user fails to give way, slow down or stop. Do not get upset or show any aggression. A good driver is patient with other road users and thus helps to ensure road safety.
When approaching a pedestrian crossing, drivers must take care to allow for pedestrians crossing, even if the yellow flashing light for pedestrians is in operation (where present).
In conclusion, the statutory provisions are complied with. If an accident can be prevented by not complying with a statutory requirement, then that course of action shall be taken. If road signs or traffic rules conflict with one another, the sign or rule that best serves road safety shall be observed.


## Driver assistance systems


When using advanced driver-assistance systems (ADAS), the following guidelines apply to ‘participating in traffic correctly and safely’:
− The driver must keep a constant eye on the car and the traffic situation whilst driving, even when using driver assistance systems.
− The driver observes the situation for themselves in real time. They make a conscious decision as to whether to use driver assistance systems.
− The driver responds to the information and warnings from the driver assistance systems in accordance with this driving procedure.
− The driver activates or deactivates the driver assistance system as required by the situation or the system itself. The term ‘situation’ refers to the road, the weather, traffic and the driver.

## 3.1 Setting off


This section covers various ways of pulling away:
− after stopping away from the traffic
− after coming to a halt in traffic.


### Driving off after stopping away from the traffic


Under the 1990 Road Traffic Regulations, pulling away from a parking position is classified as a special manoeuvre.
In doing so, other traffic must not be obstructed any more than is necessary. If disruption is nevertheless unavoidable, it must be kept to a minimum. This manoeuvre must therefore be carried out with great caution. The overall traffic situation must be assessed before setting off, and, in particular, the driver’s observation and speed must be carefully adjusted to it.
It is very important to note that:
– the speed at which other drivers are approaching and the distance between you and them
– the type of road user(s) approaching
– traffic volume, the available section of road, weather conditions and visibility.
Outside built-up areas, vehicles generally travel at higher speeds. It is therefore even more important to accurately judge the speed of oncoming traffic. Depending on these circumstances, you should take into account the short time it takes for other vehicles to approach.


#### The interests of other road users


Cause as little disruption as possible to other traffic.
This applies to all traffic, regardless of the direction from which it is approaching. This obligation therefore has a broader scope than the statutory provision requiring drivers to give way to other traffic.


#### Viewing behaviour


Look in the correct direction before and whilst setting off.
Before pulling out from the right-hand side of the road, check in the following order: rear-view mirror, straight ahead, left-hand wing mirror and over your left shoulder. Before pulling out from the left-hand side of the road, check in the following order: the interior mirror, straight ahead, the right-hand wing mirror and over your right shoulder.
When pulling out from behind an obstruction (e.g. a parked vehicle), pay extra attention to other traffic. This is due to the restricted view. If you are pulling out from such a position on the left-hand side, look over your right shoulder and into your right-hand wing mirror to ensure that you can clearly see any traffic approaching from behind. From this position, it is difficult to see traffic approaching from the front. This requires extra attention.
When pulling away, check for traffic approaching from behind using the interior and exterior mirrors.


#### Give way to


Give way to other traffic.
This obligation applies to all road users, including those approaching from the opposite direction.


#### Location on the road / scene of the incident


After setting off, take up the correct position on the carriageway.
When pulling out from behind a parked vehicle or other obstacle, maintain as smooth a path as possible, whilst keeping a safe distance from the parked vehicle or obstacle.


#### Speed


When pulling away from a parking position, do so at a safe and appropriate speed.
As soon as the vehicle has driven off, increase your speed and adjust it to match the rest of the traffic.


#### Giving/responding to signals


Once you have made sure that it is safe to drive off, indicate your intention to do so.
You must indicate your intention to change direction in good time before pulling out. The indicator must be switched off as soon as you have taken up the correct position on the carriageway.


### Pulling away after stopping in traffic


This way of pulling away is not regarded as a special manoeuvre. It is, however, a common manoeuvre that requires due attention.


#### The interests of other road users


When setting off, be mindful of other road users.
This should cause as little disruption as possible. This applies in particular to cyclists and moped riders.


#### Viewing behaviour


Take care when pulling away.
When stopped, remain alert, including to road users alongside and behind your car. When accelerating and pulling away, keep an eye on other traffic. This is to allow for sudden changes in traffic conditions, such as vehicles ahead braking or changing direction. When increasing your speed, pay particular attention to drivers overtaking you.


#### Speed


Adjust your speed as much as possible to match the rest of the traffic.
Keeping in step with other traffic helps to ensure a smooth flow of traffic.


## 3.2 Driving on straight and winding sections of road


When driving on roads, a number of factors must be taken into account, such as:
− the road layout (whether or not divided into multiple carriageways with lanes and/or whether or not equipped with cycle paths and footpaths)
− the type of road (motorway or ‘ordinary’ road)
− the location of the road (within or outside a built-up area)
− road markings, signposting, signalling, road surfacing, etc.
− other factors such as traffic volume, the weather, surrounding buildings and views.
This section deals exclusively with bends in the road. Bends encountered when turning off are covered in the section on junctions. If the situation involves a right-of-way manoeuvre where the driver is following the through road, which has right of way, then this section applies.
Some notable differences between cornering and driving on straight sections of road are:


### Perceiving and assessing a bend


By accurately and promptly assessing the curve profile, carriageway and/or lane width, road camber, the type and condition of the road surface, and so on, driving behaviour can be adjusted. Particularly on narrow roads, obstacles that obstruct visibility often occur on the inside of a bend. Accurately determining the sharpness of such a bend is then particularly difficult. Visibility of the presence of a bend and its progression is often facilitated by the ‘language of the road’ (road markings, bend signs, verge posts). Road markings and a row of trees lining the road, for example, also improve visibility.


### Visibility on the bend


Unlike on straight sections of road, visibility in bends may be restricted by the radius of the bend, combined with vegetation or buildings.
The distance between any objects that may obstruct visibility also plays a role in this. A bend is considered sufficiently clear if, before entering the bend:
− to be able to see traffic and any vehicles stationary on the bend or other obstacles
− the condition of the road surface can be assessed
− you can see the end of the bend.
Bends where visibility is insufficient should be taken at an appropriate and constant speed. Only when the bend is fully visible and the situation permits should the speed be increased.
One factor that makes a bend particularly dangerous is the presence of what is known as a ‘secondary bend’. In such cases, the radius of the bend does not remain constant, but becomes (significantly) smaller at a certain point. There is a risk that this change in radius will be noticed too late, causing the vehicle to veer off course. Speed is even more important here than usual. After all, it is usually determined on the basis of the impression gained before or as the vehicle enters the bend.


### Taking the bend


The speed when cornering is strictly limited by the centrifugal force acting on the vehicle and the frictional resistance (grip) between the tyres and the road surface.
Speed, the radius of the bend and the mass of the vehicle determine the magnitude of this centrifugal force. The tyres and the friction coefficient of the road surface on which the vehicle is travelling determine the frictional resistance (grip) between the tyres and the road surface.
As centrifugal force increases, the tyre is more likely to lose its grip on the road surface. As a result, the vehicle is unable to follow its intended line and veers off the bend.
Road verges can also play an important role on bends.
A positive camber reduces the effect of the outward-directed centrifugal force, whilst a negative camber amplifies this effect. It is particularly important to bear this in mind on narrow, winding roads and on winding approach roads to and from motorways and expressways.
This section also covers situations where oncoming drivers and drivers overtaking one another (on the left or right) are using the same carriageway. The oncoming traffic rule applies only if there is insufficient space for the vehicles to pass each other unhindered. In such a situation, both drivers have the same obligation (Section 5 of the Road Traffic Act) towards each other. Overtaking involves more than one driver. The driver who is overtaking is obliged to carry out this manoeuvre safely. The driver being overtaken is indeed a party to the situation, but is only required to react to it under certain circumstances.
The drivers involved must act in such a way as to ensure that the manoeuvre is carried out as safely as possible.


### The interests of other road users


Always drive in such a way that no danger or (as little as possible) disruption is caused, or is likely to be caused, to other road users.
Furthermore, the driver must react to the behaviour of other road users in such a way as to ensure the safest possible situation and to promote the smooth flow of traffic. When driving on a motorway or dual carriageway, under certain circumstances, drivers in a slip lane who need to merge are given assistance. This applies to a slip lane that has:
− heavy traffic that is unable to reach a sufficient speed
− several drivers who are unable to merge onto the main carriageway due to heavy traffic.
Respond to this by adjusting your speed and/or by swerving to the left.
When meeting or being overtaken, other road users are always involved, so the driver must take all those involved into account.
In this context, keeping a safe distance and maintaining a safe speed are of great importance. If you swerve to the right, you must also take into account road users who are on the right-hand side of the road.


#### Accommodation


Circumstances that may require particular attention in this regard include:
− a narrow carriageway, whether or not combined with heavy moped and bicycle traffic. Bear in mind, too, the more vulnerable road users. This often means that, in such situations, drivers have to slow down even further or even come to a complete stop
− a situation in which both drivers encounter obstacles on their respective sections of the road. In such cases, they act according to the circumstances
− the carriageway is too narrow to continue driving without difficulty. If necessary, stop and resolve the situation by mutual agreement (for example, through eye contact or hand signals).


#### Being overtaken


If, as a result of that overtaking manoeuvre, a danger or obstruction arises or is likely to arise for oncoming drivers, the driver being overtaken must endeavour to remedy that unsafe situation by:
− to reduce speed
− to swerve
− to be stopped, if necessary.
Particularly when driving at low speed in a traffic jam, you may find yourself confronted by motorcyclists overtaking. Depending on the space available, these riders will overtake on the left or the right. Be aware of this and steer in such a way that the available space is not reduced. If possible
even try to create a bit more space by moving out of the way.


### Viewing behaviour


Pay close attention to the situation in front of, beside and behind the car, as well as to how it is developing.
To this end, check your mirrors regularly, particularly in unusual road conditions such as road narrowings, merging lanes and the like.
Before slowing down and/or braking, check the rear-view mirror (and the wing mirrors if necessary) and be aware of traffic approaching from behind.
When stopping on the right-hand side of the carriageway, take great care to watch out for mopeds and bicycles travelling to your right and just behind you.
To negotiate a bend safely, you need to spot and assess that section of the road in good time.
Extra caution is required on bends where visibility is limited or poor. After all, in such situations, one is even more likely to be confronted with unexpected developments.
When meeting oncoming traffic or being overtaken, pay close attention.
Before swerving to the right, check the rear-view mirror, the right-hand wing mirror if necessary, and over your right shoulder. If you are moving to the left, check the rear-view mirror, the left-hand wing mirror and over your left shoulder. This allows you to check whether there is sufficient space and whether the manoeuvre can be carried out safely.


### Give way to


At all times, priority must be given to road users who are entitled to it (emergency vehicles, blind people, etc.)


### Location on the road / scene of the incident


Keep as far to the right as possible.
The specific circumstances on the ground determine what is meant by ‘as much law as possible’.
The driver must maintain a safe distance from the pavement, the verge, a continuous line of parked cars (the width of a car door), any oncoming traffic, and so on.
When vehicles are parked on the right-hand side at some distance from one another, do not always move to the right between these vehicles if, by not doing so, you would not be using the carriageway intended for oncoming traffic.
Take into account the presence of mopeds, bicycles and similar vehicles on the carriageway and adjust your position on the road accordingly. For example, on wide roads, there is no need to swerve to the right between mopeds and bicycles if this causes disruption to the flow of traffic. In such cases, drive in as straight a line as possible within your own half of the carriageway.
If a carriageway is divided into lanes, you should, as a general rule, keep to the right-hand lane (this also applies to open rush-hour lanes). On roads with lanes of normal width, drive in the centre of the lane. On roads where the carriageway is no wider than a single normal lane, drive in the centre of the carriageway.
If, at a particular point, a carriageway takes on the character of a junction – indicated by a chequered marking with one or more lanes on either side, above which directional signs are displayed (so-called ‘weaving lanes’), in principle the right-hand lane for the direction being taken should be used. Suggested lanes (without symbols) and cycle lanes (dashed lines) should, in principle,
Do not ride on this. Different rules may apply when turning off. These are set out in the section on junctions.
In thick fog, snowfall, driving rain, darkness and when there is spray, particularly on motorways and dual carriageways, use the right-hand lane as much as possible (to allow for an escape route).
When driving on a motorway, as you approach a slip road, check whether there is traffic on it and what options that traffic has for merging. If there are realistic opportunities for that traffic to merge, do not swerve to the left.
When negotiating bends, maintain the correct position on the carriageway by steering smoothly. This is not only important for your own driving, but also allows other road users to adapt their driving accordingly.
Taking into account obstacles, traffic facilities, etc.
When approaching another vehicle or when being overtaken, move over enough to ensure there is sufficient space between the two vehicles.


#### Accommodation


Swerve smoothly to the right. As soon as the oncoming vehicle has passed, steer smoothly to the left and return to the correct position on the carriageway. If there are obstacles on both the right and left sides of the carriageway, and there is insufficient carriageway width to allow for oncoming traffic, position yourself in such a way that you can be seen in good time.


#### Being overtaken


If a driver is being overtaken on the left, they shall move to the right, if necessary and possible. If a driver is being overtaken on the right, they shall move to the left, if necessary and possible.
If there is insufficient space alongside the vehicle for other drivers to overtake, drive in such a way as to make overtaking impossible. However, this applies only in cases where being overtaken could lead to a dangerous situation.


### Keep a safe following distance


Keep a safe distance when following another road user.
As a general rule, the ‘two-second rule’ applies here. Under this rule, the following distance is equal to the distance travelled in two seconds at a given speed.
When applying this rule, the following distance in metres is approximately half the speed in kilometres per hour. This refers to the minimum required distance between two vehicles travelling one behind the other at approximately the same speed.
If this minimum distance is maintained, then:
− if necessary, by anticipating the situation effectively, bring the vehicle to a halt in good time
− an evasive manoeuvre is carried out
− the road surface must be inspected over a sufficient distance for any irregularities and/or defects.
A greater following distance is necessary, amongst other things, when driving behind a ‘non-transparent’ vehicle ahead, in wet weather, and when another vehicle is following too closely. Incidentally, this last situation should be avoided as far as possible!
When maintaining a safe following distance, depending on road conditions and/or traffic volume, also take into account overtaking vehicles wishing to merge. In the event of a narrowing of the road, allow merging traffic the opportunity to do so.
When driving in a traffic jam, keep the distance between vehicles as constant as possible.


### Speed


Do not exceed the applicable speed limit. Furthermore, drive at a speed that does not, and cannot, cause any danger or unnecessary nuisance.
The speed must be such that the driver is at all times able to bring the vehicle to a halt within the distance over which he can see the road and which is clear.
You should also always adjust your speed to suit the situation and the other traffic on the road. This includes, amongst other things, the nature and condition of the road, weather conditions, traffic volume and roadworks.
The smaller the gap, the slower you should drive.
Do not drive unnecessarily slowly compared to other traffic unless there is a valid reason to do so. This causes unnecessary disruption and may also lead to dangerous traffic situations. When approaching a bend, speed must be adjusted to suit the road conditions and circumstances, and controlled before entering the bend in such a way that it can be negotiated safely and responsibly. On emerging from the bend, speed should be increased again as much as possible, whilst complying with the applicable speed limit.
Bends should be taken at a steady/constant speed, although in certain bends – depending on the bend radius and the situation – the speed may be increased gradually. In blind bends, do not increase your speed until you have a clear view of the entire bend.
Adjust your speed when approaching oncoming traffic. Do not increase your speed when being overtaken.
On narrower sections of road, drivers will drive at a lower speed. The extent to which speed needs to be adjusted when meeting oncoming traffic depends mainly on the width of the carriageway.


### Responding to traffic lights and instructions


Obey traffic lights. Follow instructions given by authorised officials (or other persons).
Traffic lights on straight sections of road may be installed, for example, at roadworks, on bridges, on approach roads leading to a merging lane (traffic flow control lights) and, for instance, to indicate lane changes.
Signs given by school crossing guards and traffic wardens must be obeyed.


### Responding to other road signs


Obeying road signs.
In addition to road signs which must be obeyed by law, other signs may also apply. These signs, which are placed on, alongside or above the carriageway, are intended to guide traffic or provide information about a particular situation.
Obey traffic signs or markings designed to guide traffic or improve traffic flow. This also applies to road markings which are not intended to divide a carriageway into lanes but which should nevertheless not be crossed.
Also take note of warning signs, particularly if they are positioned in relation to the road surface and the course of the road. Adjust your driving accordingly.
Compliance with, or taking account of, a road sign must always be carried out in a responsible manner. Under no circumstances should this result in, or be likely to result in, danger or more than necessary disruption to other road users.


### Giving/responding to signals


To avert imminent danger, give an audible or flashing signal.
To minimise noise and other disturbances, these signals must not last longer than is necessary. Where necessary, respond to signals given by other drivers. If, in a situation where you are giving way, you are following the course of the (through) road, it is advisable to indicate your direction to other road users for clarity. Take into account motor vehicles carrying out specific works. A potentially dangerous situation is indicated by means of a flashing light or beacons.
You should also take note of the prescribed signals used by other road users. These may include, amongst others:
− a driver signalling their intention to turn left or right
− the brake lights of a vehicle ahead that are illuminated
− flashing warning lights
− a warning triangle placed on the carriageway
− reversing lights.
Pay particular attention to signals from other road users, especially in or near blind bends.


### Slowing down, braking, stopping


If circumstances so require, slow down in good time and, if necessary, stop.
Do not brake in such a way that it causes, or is likely to cause, danger or obstruction to other road users, unless absolutely necessary.
By reducing the throttle in good time (particularly before bends), you can slow down sufficiently to avoid the need to brake. Do not brake more heavily or more frequently than is necessary given the circumstances.
If you stop, whether briefly or for a longer period, you must ensure that no danger or obstruction is caused, or is likely to be caused, to other road users. In particular, you should take into account mopeds and bicycles, which generally travel at slower speeds.


## 3.3 Behaviour near and at junctions


A junction must be approached with due care. The speed at which the junction is approached and the driver’s observation behaviour are of great importance. This not only ensures that the right-of-way rules are observed, but also enables a better response should another road user fail to give way.
At a junction, several streams of traffic converge and can continue in different directions. Some junctions are designed as small squares (mini-roundabouts). This particular design is listed under the section ‘special road sections’.
Special care is required when approaching several junctions in quick succession. For example, in the following situations:
− roads with separate carriageways
− roads with separate cycle lanes and/or footpaths
− cycle lanes and cycle/moped lanes with two-way traffic
− roads where the main carriageway is designated as a priority road, but the secondary carriageways are not.
Clear the junction as quickly as possible, in order of arrival.
Turning takes place at junctions. At some junctions, a sub-sign reading ‘deviating right of way’ is displayed beneath the ‘main road’ or ‘priority junction’ sign. In combination with specific road markings, the course of the road at that point is also emphasised. If, in a ‘deviating right of way’ situation, the course of the road indicated by the additional sign is followed, this will be regarded as driving straight ahead. However, if you deviate from the main route, you are turning off.
The preparatory actions required to turn off the road safely do not affect the specific obligations at junctions. Turning off the road usually involves negotiating a bend. In doing so, vehicle control, in conjunction with the speed at which you are travelling and your position on the road, is of the utmost importance.
Technical operation is also important.
The moment at which the decision to turn off is made must be such that all preparatory actions can be carried out in a responsible and correct manner.
Particularly at larger junctions, the lane-merging areas (marked out by road markings) are often quite long. This is not a case of merging out, but of merging into the correct lane.


### The interests of other road users


When approaching and entering a junction, always act as safely as possible. Avoid causing unnecessary obstruction.
This is the only way to respond appropriately to the incorrect behaviour of others on the road and to unexpected circumstances.
To ensure the smooth flow of traffic or to promote safer road conditions, it may be desirable or necessary to give way to or allow a road user who does not have right of way to proceed.
Although, legally speaking, pedestrians do not have to be given priority at junctions, their interests must be given due consideration in certain circumstances.
When turning off, do not cause any danger or more inconvenience than is necessary to other road users.
This applies to all traffic, regardless of where it is or from which direction it is approaching.


### Viewing behaviour


Take care when approaching and entering a junction.
When approaching a junction, determine the following at the earliest possible stage:
− the type of junction: giving or receiving right of way
− the general situation at and around that junction: the type and condition of the road surface, any special circumstances, etc.
− the view, as seen from that other driver’s perspective.
On that basis, the approach speed can be determined.
Check the mirrors to assess the situation behind the car. Just before entering the junction, check once more in the direction from which other traffic may be approaching to determine whether it is safe to enter the junction. You must focus on observing the situation carefully.
In any case, look in the following order: straight ahead, to the left, straight ahead and to the right. Repeat this as often as necessary when approaching the junction.
If the intersecting road has separate carriageways, look towards the centre of the junction before entering it to check whether there is sufficient space to manoeuvre.
At board B6 or B7, tailor your viewing behaviour even more specifically to that situation.
When preparing to turn off, look ahead in good time and in the correct manner.
Viewing method and order:
− when turning right: in the rear-view mirror, straight ahead, in the right-hand wing mirror and over your right shoulder
− when turning left: in the rear-view mirror, straight ahead, in the left-hand wing mirror and over your left shoulder.
This allows the driver to see the full traffic situation, including traffic to the right or left of the vehicle. Looking over one’s shoulder should not turn into looking backwards, or looking for too long or too often. This can cause an unwanted change of course. Moreover, the driver will then fail to see what is happening in front of the car.
Before crossing parallel roads, cycle paths or cycle lanes, unmarked paths and the like, check via the wing mirror and in your blind spot whether this section of the road can be crossed safely and without causing any obstruction. You should carry out this check whilst the car is travelling in a straight line. Extra care is required on cycle paths and similar routes where traffic travels in both directions.
When turning left, check your left-hand wing mirror once more just before entering the bend, to check for oncoming traffic.
Before turning onto the new road, check that there are no obstacles on it and that no overtaking manoeuvre is taking place on that road, which could result in a head-on collision with an oncoming vehicle.
After turning off, whilst accelerating, check the interior and exterior mirrors to keep an eye on traffic approaching from behind.


### To give way/let someone go first


Give priority to directors who are entitled to do so.
The manner of approach must be such as to give the driver with right of way confidence that he will be granted right of way.
If you stop to give way, do so in such a way that the other driver can continue on their way unhindered.
Take the turning circle into account, particularly with large vehicles. Those entitled to do so
right of way is given to other road users (emergency vehicles, blind people, trams, etc.)
When turning left or right, give way to traffic that has right of way. In certain cases, some disruption may be unavoidable whilst merging into the correct lane. This must be handled with care and consideration.
Often, a cycle and moped path forming part of the road is set back from the carriageway to such an extent that it is regarded as a separate junction. This can usually be recognised by the zigzag markings on the cycle path. In such cases, the legal obligation (regarding turning off) to give way to cyclists and moped riders does not apply.


### Location on the road / scene of the incident


Do not block an intersection.
If you are required to stop at a junction, do not drive any further than the road or traffic conditions permit.
Only drive onto the site if:
− you can carry on through or clear the junction
− it is possible to park between the intersecting streams of traffic.
If the junction is controlled by traffic lights or road signs, it may, in certain situations, be advisable to stop briefly at the junction to ensure the flow of traffic.
It is sometimes necessary to stop briefly on a cycle path or at a pedestrian crossing to check the view of the intersecting road. When doing so, do not obstruct cyclists, moped riders or pedestrians.
When turning off, it is best to move into the correct lane after checking and signalling. When negotiating the bend, take up the correct position on the carriageway.
Method of pre-sorting:
− when turning right, move as far to the right of the carriageway as possible in good time
− when turning left, move as close as possible to the kerb in good time; on a carriageway with one-way traffic, drive as far to the left as possible; and on a carriageway with restricted one-way traffic, drive as close as possible to the kerb.
Lane selection is required in situations where, if lanes are not selected:
− an unsafe situation arises or is likely to arise
− the smooth flow of traffic is, or may be, impeded.
For example
− by moving into the left-hand lane in advance when turning left, you prevent traffic approaching from behind from overtaking on the left, which could lead to undesirable situations involving oncoming traffic
− by moving into the correct lane before turning left, this often creates space for traffic behind to overtake on the right
− By moving into the right-hand lane when turning right, you prevent traffic behind you from being held up more than necessary when overtaking.
If the carriageway is divided into several lanes and there is a merging lane, move into the adjacent lane in good time and enter the merging lane from there. Sometimes it is better not to merge in advance. This is to avoid unnecessarily obstructing cyclists and moped riders when driving slowly or stopping in the lane where you have already merged.
Stopping in a pre-selected position perpendicular to the carriageway can be particularly dangerous on country roads. It is better to avoid situations of this kind.


#### Cycle lane (broken line)


Although the use of this lane by drivers other than cyclists or
Although vehicles for disabled people are not prohibited by law, that lane remains, in effect, a part of the carriageway intended for cyclists. Other road users should therefore only use this lane when a particular traffic situation makes it advisable or necessary to do so. Under no circumstances should cyclists be obstructed. Stopping in a cycle lane should be avoided as far as possible.


#### Suggestion strip (without symbols)


This is a section of the carriageway which is intended for use by cyclists and vehicles for the disabled. This lane may be used when turning right.


#### Traffic signs


Respect markings that indicate bends, deflection surfaces, droplets and the like, or any markings that promote better traffic flow. This also applies to continuous lines that do not divide the carriageway into lanes, such as kerb lines.
If you are forced to stop whilst turning left due to oncoming traffic, keep the junction clear. In doing so, keep the car’s front wheels in the ‘straight-ahead position’ as much as possible.
The obligation to keep the junction clear does not apply if traffic at that point is controlled by traffic lights or if traffic on the road in question has right of way over crossing traffic. If, when turning left, the road you are joining consists of two or more lanes, you must enter the correct lane.
Where oncoming traffic is also turning left at the same junction, you should preferably let each other pass unless road markings indicate otherwise.
Notes: The shark’s teeth markings intended for drivers joining the junction are not intended to guide traffic. When negotiating the bend, drivers must maintain or move into the correct position on the carriageway using a smooth steering manoeuvre.
When turning towards:
− keep to the right as far as possible
− on the left, drive in such a way as to end up on the right-hand side of the road. Avoid ‘cutting the corner’
− Turn left into a narrow street; only enter once you have a clear view, due to oncoming traffic.


### Keep a safe following distance


When turning off, keep a safe distance so that you can stop in time. If a vehicle in front appears to be about to turn off, keep an extra safe distance.


### Speed


Approach and enter a junction at a safe and sensible speed.
The approach speed is correct if, under all circumstances, the right of way can be observed in the normal manner and traffic lights, road signs and/or instructions can be followed.
Approach junctions that are close to one another at as constant a speed as possible. Avoid driving at constantly changing speeds. Even when approaching junctions where you have right of way, adjust your speed if necessary. This is particularly important if traffic on the minor road, due to excessive speed or other reasons, does not appear to be giving way.
At sign B6, adjust your speed even more carefully to suit the situation. At sign B7, approach at a speed that allows you to stop safely.
When turning off, drive at a speed that allows the manoeuvre to be carried out safely.
Speed must be adjusted to suit the overall traffic situation. For example:
− the bend is taken correctly
− the obligation to give way to traffic with right of way is complied with
− be stopped in good time (if necessary).
If you need to reduce your speed, this should generally be done whilst in the correct position and before entering the bend. After the bend, adjust your speed to suit the conditions.


### Responding to traffic lights and instructions


Obey the traffic lights for your chosen direction of travel. Follow instructions given by authorised officials.
Do not stop or slow down if you are required to proceed through a yellow traffic light, if you have approached the signal so closely that stopping or slowing down would create, or could create, a danger to traffic approaching from behind. Extra caution is required at traffic lights where the amber light is flashing.
Signals given by traffic wardens must be obeyed.


### Responding to other road signs


Obey road signs which must be followed in accordance with the law.
If, at a junction, the direction to follow or the manner of driving to be observed is indicated by road signs, you must follow these. You must also take into account warning signs such as ‘dangerous junction’ and similar. If sign B7 is present, stop before the stop line. If there is no stop line, stop at a point from which you have the best possible view of the junction and can enter it safely.
When turning off, follow any signs indicating a requirement or prohibition.


#### Pre-sorting lane


When merging, use the merging lane on the carriageway.
Where there are several lanes available in one direction, the choice of lane is determined by the volume of traffic and the situation on the road.
For example, the left-hand lane is preferably used if there is a lot of heavy goods traffic, or traffic travelling more slowly, in the right-hand lane. The choice of lane is also based on which lane will be taken after turning off.
Once you have chosen a lane, stay in it as much as possible. This helps to keep traffic flowing smoothly. Vehicles that are stationary or travelling slowly in another lane may be overtaken on the right.
If a lane for merging is intended for all drivers travelling in that direction, position your vehicle in such a way that there remains sufficient space to the right of it for cyclists and moped riders.


### Giving/responding to signals


When turning, indicate in good time and after checking that it is safe to do so.
‘In good time’ means: at a point in time that allows other road users to become aware of your intention to turn well in advance and to adjust their own driving behaviour accordingly. Once you have completed the turn, stop indicating.
If you are not following the main road and are, in fact, turning off it, you must indicate your intention to do so. However, depending on the road conditions, you must do this in such a way that there is no confusion for other road users.
Signals from other drivers are responded to promptly and appropriately.


### Slowing down, braking, stopping


If circumstances so require, slow down in good time and stop if necessary.
This obligation applies, for example, if another driver fails to give way. In such a case, do not carry on driving, but try to prevent a collision and resolve the situation as safely as possible.
Do not brake in such a way that it causes, or is likely to cause, danger or obstruction to other road users. This obligation means, for example, that you must not give way unnecessarily to road users who do not have right of way. Always slow down, brake or stop in an appropriate manner, and do not brake harder or for longer than is necessary.


## 3.4 Insert – Remove


When merging, it is important that the speed of traffic entering the main carriageway from the merging lane is virtually the same as the speed of traffic on that main carriageway. Proper use of the slip road enables drivers merging into the main carriageway to adjust their speed to match that of the traffic on the main carriageway.
Under normal circumstances, drivers only slow down on the exit lane. This allows traffic on the main carriageway to continue unhindered.
An acceleration or deceleration lane is a section of road separated from the main carriageway by road markings.
Without a merging lane, joining and leaving the main carriageway would, in many cases, lead to dangerous situations. This is particularly true on motorways and expressways, where traffic usually travels at high speeds. Merging from an emergency lane, parking lane or car park is carried out in the same way as described in this chapter. When merging into or exiting the main carriageway, a number of manoeuvres must be carried out correctly so as not to cause any danger or obstruction to other traffic.
The main actions are:
− viewing behaviour
− controlling the speed
− the correct use of the available road space.
Proper use of the acceleration lane makes it easier to merge onto the main carriageway without obstructing traffic on that carriageway. Proper use of the exit lane makes it easier to leave the main carriageway, so that traffic on the main carriageway can continue unhindered.
There are also merging lanes combined with an exit lane. In this situation, both traffic wishing to merge and traffic on the through carriageway wishing to exit make use of this combined merging and exit lane.
On this stretch of road, it sometimes happens that, when approaching from a feeder road and driving in the combined entry and exit lane, drivers neither merge into the main carriageway nor exit it.
In such cases, the driver simply needs to continue along the road, whilst remaining aware of the specific nature of that section of the road. Driving behaviour – in particular, the driver’s line of sight and speed – should be adapted accordingly, especially at the point where the combined lane and the main carriageway merge.


### The interests of other road users


This must not cause any inconvenience to other drivers.
Merge into the lane in the order in which you and the other drivers entered the merge lane. This ensures you do not take up space that a vehicle ahead has already anticipated.
When driving in the merging/exit lane, do not overtake vehicles on the main carriageway unnecessarily, particularly if there is no escape route. To ensure the smooth flow of traffic, overtaking via a combined entry/exit lane may be desirable or necessary, for example due to the road layout or traffic volume. Unnecessary disruption and/or danger should be avoided.


#### Comments


When overtaking on the right on entry and exit lanes, bear in mind the following, amongst other things:
− the loss of sight lines (left-hand lane)
− the length of the acceleration or deceleration lane
− the difference in speed between them
− the driving behaviour and position of other drivers.
When using a combined entry/exit lane, be aware of traffic exiting the motorway both before and whilst merging.
When driving along a short combined entry/exit lane (e.g. the link between two quadrants of a cloverleaf junction), merge as quickly as possible. This makes way for drivers wishing to exit.
An access road may also merge directly into a lane forming part of the main carriageway. When joining that lane, be aware of traffic on the main carriageway that may swerve to the right.


### Viewing behaviour


Take care when merging into and exiting traffic.


#### Insert


It is important to observe the traffic on the main carriageway as early as possible whilst approaching the slip road. This allows you to determine whether there are opportunities to merge. Whilst driving on the merge lane (whether or not combined with an exit lane), regularly check the situation behind your car by looking in your mirrors.
Just before joining the main carriageway, also look over your left shoulder to check that there are no vehicles in the so-called ‘blind spot’.
Ideally, you should only look over your shoulder when there is a genuine opportunity to merge. This ensures you can still see any traffic ahead of the merging driver that may be slowing down or braking.
Looking behind you must not turn into looking back too often or for an unnecessarily long time. This can cause the car to veer off course. Moreover, you will then fail to see what is happening in front of the car.
If merging into a stationary or very slow-moving traffic jam, ‘ask’ for permission to do so at the start of the merge lane by making eye contact with the drivers concerned and using your indicators.
When entering the combined entry and exit lane, if you do not merge but continue to drive on the right-hand side – as it were, to exit straight away – you should look in the same way as when merging.
When the slip road merges into the main carriageway, pay particular attention to drivers moving to the right.


#### Unfold


It is important to monitor traffic on the exit lane (possibly combined with a merging lane) from the through carriageway at the earliest possible stage.
When approaching a right-hand exit lane, check the rear-view mirror, the right-hand wing mirror and over your right shoulder. This allows you to spot traffic approaching from behind that also wishes to merge. When approaching a left-hand exit lane, check your rear-view mirror, left-hand wing mirror and over your left shoulder. This will allow you to spot any traffic approaching from behind that also wishes to merge.
To ensure that the manoeuvre can be carried out safely, look again as described above just before leaving the main carriageway. Looking over your shoulder must not turn into looking backwards, looking over your shoulder too often or for an unnecessarily long time. This can cause an unwanted change of direction. Moreover, you will then fail to see what is happening in front of the car.
If you are merging out of a stationary or very slow-moving traffic jam (often on a combined entry and exit lane), ‘ask’ for permission to do so at the start of the exit lane by making eye contact with the drivers concerned. Do this whilst using your indicator.


### Give way to


When joining the main carriageway, give way to traffic already on that carriageway. When merging, give way to traffic on the exit lane.
The law requires both drivers merging into traffic and those exiting traffic to give way to other road users. This provision can cause problems on a combined merge and exit lane.
In such a situation, the basic principle is therefore that drivers merging into the main carriageway must give way to drivers already on it. This allows traffic on the main carriageway to continue unhindered.
On a combined entry/exit lane, the differences in speed between vehicles are often considerable. Being considerate of one another and anticipating the situation are therefore even more important than usual.


### Location on the road / scene of the incident


Use the acceleration lane for as long as necessary. Merge onto the main carriageway at the start of the deceleration lane.


#### Insert


Even if there is no traffic on the main carriageway, you should still use the entire length of the acceleration lane. This allows you to check the road ahead as required and increase your speed.
Merging from the slip road onto the main carriageway should be done in a smooth, continuous motion. If, due to heavy traffic on the main carriageway, you need to stop on the slip road, you should preferably stop on the first section of that slip road. The remaining section can then still be used to merge correctly.
It is not permitted to move directly from the acceleration lane into a lane other than the right-hand lane in a single manoeuvre. If the acceleration lane merges into an emergency lane, you should, as a general rule, not use that lane when merging.
Once the combined merging and exit lane has been marked on the road surface with arrows pointing to the right, you should, in principle, not use this lane to merge.
You should avoid following too closely behind another vehicle travelling in the through lane.


#### Unfold


Before exiting: move into the lane adjacent to the exit lane in good time (approximately 600 metres beforehand). From that position, after carrying out the preparatory actions (looking and signalling), merge onto the exit lane.
Only a vehicle travelling very slowly at the start of the exit lane may, where the exit lane is long, still be overtaken via the main carriageway.
When joining the exit lane from the main carriageway, drivers should do so in a smooth, continuous manoeuvre. Drivers should avoid merging too closely behind another vehicle already in the exit lane.
When leaving the main carriageway via the hard shoulder, you must not enter the exit lane.
In the case of a combined entry and exit lane, it may happen that it is not possible to exit at the start of that lane due to traffic on that lane. In such cases, where the lane is of considerable length, drivers may continue along the main carriageway. However, this is only permitted if there is a possibility to exit elsewhere along that lane. Stopping on the main carriageway must be avoided. An exit lane may consist of two adjacent lanes. The use of the left-hand lane depends on the direction of travel and the volume of traffic.


### Keep a safe following distance


Keep a safe distance when merging into and exiting traffic.
Particularly on a combined entry and exit lane, keep your distance and ensure that ‘weaving’ is possible. The following distance is partly determined by the type of vehicle being followed and the speed at which you are travelling. When merging, which often involves a significant reduction in speed, keeping a safe distance is very important.


### Speed


Adjust your speed on the acceleration lane to match the traffic on the main carriageway as closely as possible. In principle, maintain the speed at which traffic is travelling on the main carriageway right up to the exit lane.


#### Insert


You should also adjust your speed on the acceleration lane to match that of the vehicles ahead.
The first opportunity to merge may be taken if your speed has been sufficiently adjusted to match the traffic on the main carriageway. In the event of slow-moving traffic in a tailback, adjust your speed so that, where possible, you do not need to stop on the merging lane.
Even when joining the main carriageway via a combined entry and exit lane, adjust your speed as much as possible to match the traffic on the main carriageway and the traffic exiting the junction.
When joining the main carriageway of a motorway or expressway from the slip road at a time when no other drivers, or virtually no other drivers, are approaching, the speed should, as far as possible, be in line with the speed that is customary on that road.
If you are not merging whilst using the combined entry/exit lane, be aware of traffic wishing to exit from the main carriageway and adjust your speed accordingly.


#### Unfold


On the exit lane, adjust your speed further if necessary. Reducing your speed on the main carriageway is permitted, amongst other things, if:
− the exit lane does not provide sufficient opportunity (due to its length) to slow down
− other circumstances require the speed to be adjusted
− if traffic conditions allow you to reduce the throttle (energy-efficient driving).
Where applicable, this should be done as gradually as possible. In the case of a combined entry/exit lane, the speed should also be adjusted to suit the overall situation. The length of this lane and the volume of traffic are key factors in this regard.


### Giving/responding to signals


Signal well in advance before merging from the acceleration lane onto the main carriageway. When exiting, whilst driving in the right-hand lane, signal well in advance (approximately 300 metres beforehand).


#### Insert


Giving directions must never be used as a means of coercion.
On combined entry/exit lanes, it may be necessary to indicate your direction earlier to allow other road users to anticipate the situation more effectively. Stop indicating as soon as the entire vehicle has crossed the block marking.
If a driver does not merge when entering a combined merging and exit lane, they must indicate right shortly before the point where the merging and exit lane joins the main carriageway.
When driving in a slip-road, watch out for drivers approaching from behind on the main carriageway who
Indicate a turn to the left. If they move over to the left, you can take advantage of the opportunity to merge.
Particularly on combined entry/exit lanes, keep an eye out for signs from drivers exiting the motorway. This will enable you to react appropriately to them.


#### Unfold


Switch off the indicator at the point where the lane branches off from the main carriageway. On an exit lane, a longer combined entry and exit lane, or a multi-lane exit lane, you may switch off your indicator earlier. To ensure clarity for other road users, it may be advisable to indicate again part-way along the exit lane and shortly before the end of the lane.


### Slowing down, braking, stopping


If circumstances so require, slow down in good time and stop if necessary.
Do not brake in such a way, unless necessary, that it causes or is likely to cause a danger or obstruction to other road users.
The braking method must always be adapted to the situation in question.


## 3.5 Overtaking / moving sideways


Overtaking and changing lanes are covered in this section. These manoeuvres are very similar and relate to the same basic type of behaviour.
Overtaking is carried out by road users themselves. Obstacles are not overtaken, but are simply passed.
Obstacles include, amongst other things:
− parked vehicles
− containers and barriers on the carriageway
− other objects that are not part of the traffic.
Overtaking is a manoeuvre that warrants particular attention. This is because it usually involves deviating from the basic rule of keeping as far to the right as possible.
Overtaking also usually involves using a different part of the carriageway. This restricts the options available to other road users. This can easily cause disruption or even pose a danger.
An overtaking manoeuvre should not take longer than is necessary. The driver overtaking must return to their normal position on the road as soon as possible. Overtaking must be possible and safe in the context of the overall traffic situation.
Key criteria for assessing this include, amongst others:
− the road conditions (width, surface, etc.)
− the ability to pick up speed so that the manoeuvre takes as little time as possible
− the clarity of the road
− the available space, taking into account, amongst other things, other traffic
− the speed of the road user to be overtaken.
As a general rule, overtaking must be carried out on the left. In certain situations, overtaking may (only) be carried out on the right.
The section on changing lanes and moving sideways covers movements that are not directly related to manoeuvres such as turning off, overtaking, and merging into or exiting traffic.
Changing lanes and other lateral movements are special manoeuvres.
Changing lanes or moving sideways are manoeuvres that require particular attention. They can be carried out in either a left-hand or right-hand direction.
On carriageways that are not divided into lanes, there can be no question of changing lanes, but rather of moving sideways.
Depending on the extent to which a lateral manoeuvre is carried out, this may pose a danger or cause disruption to other road users.


### The interests of other road users


No danger or nuisance must arise, or be likely to arise, for other road users.
This obligation applies to all road users, regardless of:
− where they are located
− from which direction they are approaching
− how they (may) be involved in the overtaking manoeuvre.


#### Overtaking


When overtaking a vehicle whose driver has a restricted view to the rear, ensure that the driver can see the overtaking vehicle in good time.
For example, this means:
− on motorways and dual carriageways, leave your own lane sooner
− keep a greater distance on single-carriageway roads
− make eye contact with the driver you are about to overtake via the mirrors.
When overtaking on the right in traffic jams or at a lane marking, continue to follow the chosen lane as far as possible.
When overtaking on the right, take extra care to:
− drivers changing to the right-hand lane
− drivers wishing to enter the exit lane
− drivers who overtake on the left and then wish to merge back into traffic (particularly on combined entry/exit lanes).
After overtaking, do not move into the right-hand lane when approaching a slip road on which vehicles are merging.
When changing lanes or moving sideways, no danger or obstruction must be caused, or be likely to be caused, to other road users.
Changing lanes and, in doing so, taking a position for which another driver is clearly preparing is not permitted.


### Viewing behaviour


Before overtaking, check whether it can be done safely and responsibly.


#### Overtaking


When overtaking on the left, if there is a lateral movement, look in good time as follows:
− forwards
− in the rear-view mirror
− in the left-hand wing mirror
− over the left shoulder.
When overtaking on the right, if there is a lateral movement, look in good time as follows:
− forwards
− in the rear-view mirror
− in the right-hand wing mirror
− over the right shoulder.
Just before swerving, ‘check’ once more for any traffic approaching from behind by:
When overtaking on the left, proceed as follows:
− forwards
− in the rear-view mirror
− in the left-hand wing mirror
− over the left shoulder.
When overtaking on the right, look as follows:
− forwards
− in the rear-view mirror
− in the right-hand wing mirror
− over the right shoulder.
Looking over your shoulder is only intended to check the so-called ‘blind spot’ next to the vehicle. It must not turn into looking backwards or looking over your shoulder too often or for an unnecessarily long time. This can cause an unwanted change in direction. Furthermore, you cannot see what is happening in front of the car. When overtaking, pay attention to the road user you are overtaking, amongst other things.
When returning to the correct position on the carriageway after overtaking, first check the rear-view mirror and also:
− before turning right, check the right-hand wing mirror and look over your right shoulder
− before turning left, check the left-hand wing mirror and look over your left shoulder.


#### Passing by


When passing an obstacle at such a moment, look to ensure that, if it is not possible to swerve sufficiently to the left, you can still come to a halt well before the obstacle. Before setting off again after such a stop, check whether you can do so without endangering or inconveniencing others. Do this by looking as follows:
− forwards
− in the rear-view mirror
− in the left-hand wing mirror
− over the left shoulder
Before setting off, also ‘check’ the traffic to the right of the vehicle.
If you do not need to stop, just before swerving to the left, check the rear-view mirror, the left-hand wing mirror and over your left shoulder. This will allow you to spot any oncoming traffic.
When moving back to the right after overtaking, first look over your right shoulder and check the right-hand wing mirror. This is particularly important in situations where there is, or may be, moped or bicycle traffic to the right of the vehicle.
Take care before changing lanes or making any other lateral manoeuvre.
When turning left, look as follows:
− forwards
− in the rear-view mirror
− in the left-hand wing mirror
− over the left shoulder.
When turning right, look as follows:
− forwards
− in the rear-view mirror
− in the right-hand wing mirror
− over the right shoulder.
A driver who regularly keeps a close watch on traffic approaching from behind (as described in the section on ‘driving on straight and winding stretches of road’) need not look over their shoulder if there is only a slight lateral movement.
Looking over your shoulder must not turn into looking back, or looking over your shoulder too often or for too long. This can cause the car to veer off course. Moreover, you will not be able to see what is happening in front of the car.


### Give way to


If, whilst overtaking or passing, you reach a section of the road intended for other traffic, you must give way to that traffic.
The requirement to give way also applies to traffic that the driver who is overtaking or passing is themselves overtaking.
When changing lanes or moving sideways, give way to other road users.


### Location on the road/scene of the incident


Before, during and after overtaking, take up the correct position on the carriageway. In certain situations or circumstances, do not overtake.


#### Overtaking


Overtaking takes place on the left. In certain circumstances, overtaking on the right is permitted, recommended or required.
Overtaking: ‘The correct position on the carriageway’ refers to a position that allows the road user being overtaken to be overtaken safely.
The spacing between them plays an important role here.
In this regard, the following points, amongst others, are important:
− the type of vehicle being overtaken
− the type of road user being overtaken (children, the elderly, people with disabilities)
− the available section of road
− other traffic
− the speed of the vehicle being overtaken
− the speed at which you are driving
− the weather conditions and the view.
When overtaking at such a time, move out of the way in good time so that you continue driving straight ahead for a short while before reaching the rear of the vehicle you are overtaking.
Carry out the overtaking manoeuvre in a smooth, continuous line.
After overtaking, the driver must not return to their own section of the road or lane until the entire front of the vehicle they have overtaken is visible in the rear-view mirror. This ensures that the normal passage of that driver is not obstructed. Road situations where, in particular, vehicles with more than two wheels are not to be overtaken include:
− confusing junctions consisting of roads of the same priority where traffic is not controlled by traffic lights
− junctions on priority roads where the view of a side road on the right is obstructed by the vehicle in front (which is to be overtaken)
− blind bends and gradients, unless the carriageway is divided into lanes and, when overtaking, you do not enter a lane intended for oncoming traffic
− railway crossings fitted only with a flashing light system.
It is precisely in these situations that the driver overtaking may encounter other road users.
At and near (pedestrian) crossings, overtaking can pose an additional risk due to the restricted view.


#### Passing by


The following specific provisions apply when passing:
− swerve in a smooth arc
− if necessary, stop at a point where it is possible to continue driving in a smooth, continuous line afterwards
− as soon as you have passed the obstacle, steer smoothly to the right.
When passing, keep a minimum gap of at least one car door’s width wherever possible.
If there are several obstacles within a relatively short distance of one another, do not veer to the right unnecessarily, unless this is necessary, for example, to avoid oncoming traffic.
When driving between obstacles on the right and left of the carriageway, keep the smallest possible gap on the left-hand side. That side is the easiest for the driver to see.
Changing lanes or moving sideways should be done smoothly.
When changing lanes (where required), the manoeuvre must be carried out in good time. It is not permitted to move laterally across several lanes in a single manoeuvre. This must always be done one lane at a time.
On a motorway, you must always have been driving straight ahead for a short while before changing lanes (again).
Where there are several lanes side by side for traffic travelling in the same direction, continue in the chosen lane when approaching a bend. After the bend and once you have been travelling straight ahead for a short while, you may change lanes. Changing lanes or moving sideways should be done in a smooth, continuous motion.


### Speed


Overtaking should be carried out at a speed that ensures the manoeuvre is kept as brief as possible. When passing obstacles, adjust your speed to suit the situation.


#### Overtaking


Particularly on carriageways with traffic travelling in both directions, a overtaking manoeuvre carried out too slowly can pose a danger to other road users. This is because this way of driving results in the section of the road intended for oncoming traffic being occupied for longer than necessary.
In preparation for overtaking, depending on the situation, increase your speed as soon as there is an opportunity to overtake. Do not wait until you have changed lanes or until you have already started to move sideways.
Also take into account the speed at which you are travelling; this relates to the distance to be maintained (on the left and right). This applies particularly on cycle lanes where there is traffic. The narrower the gap, the smaller the speed difference should be when overtaking. This applies in particular to certain categories of road users.
Two-wheeled vehicles in particular – especially when ridden by, for example, the elderly, children and people with disabilities – are less stable. When overtaking these road users, it is necessary to adjust your speed depending on the available space. This also applies when overtaking agricultural tractors and other motor vehicles with limited speed.
If, whilst overtaking, it becomes apparent that you need to slow down, ease off the accelerator in good time so as to avoid having to brake as far as possible.


#### Passing by


With regard to the speed to be adjusted, the following aspects are important when overtaking:
− carriageway width, visibility and the presence and behaviour of other road users often make it necessary to reduce speed
− if the required distance of at least one door’s width cannot be maintained, the speed is reduced even further.


#### Changing lanes


If, when changing lanes, your speed has been adjusted sufficiently to match that of the other traffic, you may change lanes.


### Responding to other road signs


Do not overtake where this is prohibited by road signs.
Please also take note of other road signs, such as those at pedestrian crossings, dangerous junctions and bends. You must also observe the road markings.
When overtaking, it is sometimes necessary to act in contravention of a traffic sign. These are exceptional cases where the flow of traffic is at risk. In such situations, disruption to others must be kept to a minimum. For example: a parked lorry unloading next to a solid white line.
Do not change lanes if this is prohibited by road signs.
Please also take into account other signs, such as those indicating a narrowing of the road.


### Giving/responding to signals


If overtaking involves a significant lateral movement, indicate your intention.


#### Overtaking


Always indicate when overtaking a motor vehicle. Give a signal in good time after checking that it is safe to overtake or pass.
‘In good time’ here means: at a point in time when you are still driving in a straight line and other road users are given early warning of the overtaking manoeuvre. Begin overtaking shortly after indicating.
The situation on the ground determines whether a lateral movement should be regarded as significant. For example, overtaking a cyclist on a narrow road can sometimes be regarded as a significant lateral movement.
Before returning to the correct position on the carriageway after overtaking, indicate your intention.


#### Passing by


When overtaking, too, the situation (type of obstacle, carriageway width, etc.) determines whether there is a significant lateral displacement.
After overtaking, signal your intention before returning to your correct position on the carriageway. Whether overtaking or passing, you should stop signalling once the lateral manoeuvre is complete.
Before changing lanes or making any other significant lateral manoeuvre, indicate your intention.
Signal your intention only after checking that the lane change or other significant lateral manoeuvre can be carried out without endangering or inconveniencing other road users, and well before changing lanes or beginning a lateral manoeuvre.
Stop signalling as soon as you have completed the lane change or other lateral manoeuvre.
What constitutes a significant lateral movement depends on the situation. For example, a small movement on a narrow road is more likely to be regarded as significant than if the same movement were to take place on a wide carriageway.


### Slowing down, braking, stopping


If circumstances so require, slow down in good time and stop if necessary.
Do not brake in such a way, unless necessary, that it causes or is likely to cause a danger or obstruction to other road users.
Always adapt your braking to the situation at hand. Do not brake more forcefully than is necessary given the circumstances.


## 3.6 Behaviour near and on specific sections of road


Special road sections are sections of road which, due to their function and/or layout, differ significantly from other road sections.
Drivers approaching or driving on such a stretch of road are generally also expected to adopt a different driving style, one that is better suited to that particular situation.
In these procedures, the following are defined as special road sections:
− entrance/exit
− yard
− on the move
− pedestrian crossing
− tram/bus stop
− roundabout.


### Entrance/exit


Driving onto or off an entrance/exit is considered a special manoeuvre, during which other road users must be given way.
During this manoeuvre, one may encounter:
− traffic approaching from the left or right
− oncoming traffic, traffic to the left or right of the car, or traffic approaching from behind
− traffic wishing to enter the exit
− traffic wishing to exit the slip road.
If you are driving away from a situation that resembles an entrance or exit, treat it as you would an entrance or exit.


#### The interests of other road users


Do not obstruct other traffic.
In this context, it is irrelevant where this traffic is located, from which direction it is approaching, or which category of road users it belongs to.
The driver pulling out may also encounter traffic wishing to enter the exit. In such cases, both drivers are legally obliged to give way to one another. In this situation, it will sometimes be necessary to act in accordance with the circumstances.


#### Viewing behaviour


Keep a close watch out for traffic coming from the left, the right or oncoming traffic.
This also applies to traffic on, for example, cycle paths or footpaths. When joining the carriageway, check your mirrors to check for traffic approaching from behind.


#### Give way to


Give way to other traffic.
Unlike when giving way, the obligation to give way to other road users also applies here in relation to pedestrians.
The driver exiting the junction may also encounter drivers wishing to enter it. The driver entering the junction may also encounter drivers wishing to exit it. In both cases, the drivers are legally obliged to give way to one another. In this situation, it is preferable to give way to the driver exiting the junction. This improves traffic flow and will make it easier to enter the junction.


#### Location on the road / scene of the incident


When leaving the exit, take up the correct position on the carriageway as soon as possible, keeping a smooth line. When entering the slip road, get into the correct lane as described for turning off.


##### Exit


Regardless of the road conditions and the position of the exit in relation to the main road, when turning right, take the bend in such a way that you move into the correct position on the carriageway in one smooth manoeuvre. When turning left, take the bend wide enough to end up on the right-hand side of the carriageway.
Unless it is possible to position your vehicle between the crossing streams of traffic (e.g. on carriageways with separate lanes), do not join the road until you can continue driving and the crossing section of the road can be cleared.


##### Entrance


In certain cases (see ‘turning off’), it is better not to move into the correct lane in advance.


#### Speed


When approaching an entrance or exit, drive at a safe and appropriate speed.
As soon as you have left the exit, adjust your speed to match the flow of traffic as quickly as possible.


#### Giving/responding to signals


If you are joining the road from the left or right, indicate your intention before leaving the exit. If you are turning into the road from the left or right, indicate your intention in good time.
Switch off the indicator as soon as you have moved into the correct position on the carriageway.


#### Slowing down, braking, stopping


If circumstances so require: brake in good time and stop if necessary.
Do not brake in such a way, unless necessary, that it causes or is likely to cause a danger or obstruction to other road users.


### Yard


A farmyard is primarily intended as a place to stay. The roads within a farmyard are not intended for through traffic.
All road users are permitted to use a courtyard and have an equal right to do so. For example, pedestrians are allowed to move about freely anywhere within a courtyard.
All of this means that defensive driving is of the utmost importance within a farmyard. Vehicle drivers must be constantly aware of the unique nature of the farmyard.
The entrances and exits of a property usually take the form of a driveway. Sign G5 is placed at the entrances. Exits are marked with sign G6. There are also situations where the exit from a property is situated some distance from the main road. The junction with that main road then takes the form of a crossroads.


#### The interests of other road users


Adapt your driving style to the specific purpose of the yard and the behaviour of those using it.
In this way, the driver’s driving behaviour shows that they are willing to be considerate of other road users.
At any point within the grounds, the driver may encounter other road users, ranging from elderly people out for a walk to children playing. After all, they are entitled to use the entire grounds.


#### Viewing behaviour


Always keep a close eye on your surroundings when you’re in a courtyard.
It is important to keep your field of vision as wide and as far-reaching as possible.
Only then is it possible to react appropriately, for example, if a child suddenly runs out into the road.
During manoeuvres such as:
− approaching and entering a junction
− turn off
− When performing a lateral movement, the procedure described in the relevant section must be followed.


#### Right of way/giving way


Give way to or allow priority to road users who are entitled to it.
The manner of approach must be such as to give the driver with right of way confidence that he will be granted right of way.
If you stop to give way or allow another road user to pass, you must stop in such a way that other road users can continue on their way (unhindered).


#### Location on the road/scene of the incident


Keep to the right as much as possible.
This depends, in part, on the layout of the yard. It is always advisable to position yourself on the road in a way that is as safe as possible, given the circumstances.


#### Speed


Drive at a maximum of 15 km/h within a farmyard.
This enables safe driving behaviour that is adapted to pedestrian traffic. Although pedestrians must not obstruct the normal progress of motor vehicles, they are only given the opportunity to react appropriately to those vehicles when drivers are travelling very slowly. By driving at a maximum speed of 15 km/h, the driver is able to react appropriately to whatever situation they encounter.


#### Responding to other road signs


Obey road signs which must be followed in accordance with the law.
When entering a courtyard, you will pass sign G5. It is vital that motor vehicle drivers, in particular, realise that they are entering a special section of road. Driving behaviour must therefore be adapted accordingly at all times.


#### Giving/responding to signals


Give signals in the same way as you would outside the farmyard.
Given the unique nature of a private driveway, particular attention must be paid to signals from other drivers.


#### Slowing down, braking, stopping


If necessary, brake and stop in good time.


### Crossing


A level crossing must be approached and crossed with caution. Accidents at level crossings are usually very serious.
It is very important to take into account the glare caused by the low-lying sun. This can render the red light at the level crossing completely invisible. A level crossing may be protected in various ways. Drivers must be aware that warning and safety equipment may malfunction.
In addition, markers may be placed at level crossings. The distance to the level crossing can be determined by counting the number of diagonal stripes on these markers (each stripe indicates a distance of approximately 80 metres).


#### The interests of other road users


Exercise particular caution when approaching and driving over a level crossing.
Refrain from any actions that cause or may cause danger or a nuisance in any way.
This applies in particular where there is other traffic, or at a level crossing of a special nature or design (narrow, unguarded).
When approaching a level crossing, particularly in situations where visibility is poor, be aware that the barriers may be down and that there may be a stationary traffic jam.


#### Viewing behaviour


When approaching a level crossing, keep a close watch from as early a stage as possible.
This makes it possible, partly by looking at the signs and other indicators, to determine in good time what type of level crossing you are approaching. To make sure that no train is approaching, look carefully to the left and right before driving onto a level crossing.
Once a train has passed, before driving onto the tracks, check carefully to make sure that another train is not approaching from the other direction.


#### Give way to


At level crossings, road users must give way to rail vehicles.


#### Location on the road/scene of the incident


Stopping on a level crossing is prohibited. You may only drive onto it if you can continue through and clear the crossing completely.
It is particularly important to be aware of this if there is a junction after the level crossing or if you are driving in a traffic jam.
Engining at level crossings is a common occurrence and can have serious consequences. As there is often a gradient here, the risk of this happening is greater. It is vital to minimise this risk as much as possible. Consider making use of the car’s momentum and ensure that the most critical moment of pulling away is some distance from the level crossing.


#### Speed


Approach and drive onto a level crossing at the appropriate speed.
This applies particularly to level crossings that are unguarded or inadequately secured. Adjusting speed is linked, amongst other things, to:
− the type of level crossing (whether or not it is protected)
− the view
− unevenness of the road surface
− the carriageway width.


#### Responding to traffic lights and instructions


Obey the traffic lights for the chosen direction of travel.
Obey the signals given by the railway vehicle attendant.
A white flashing light means: no train is approaching. A red flashing light means: stop.
With automatic half-barrier level crossings (AHOB), the red light only goes out once the barriers have fully
raised. Only then may you drive onto the level crossing. In other situations, you may only drive onto a level crossing once the red light has gone out.
A yellow flashing light (possibly with an arrow) indicates a danger spot. Caution is advised. An escort may signal to stop using a sign, a red flag or a red light.
In the case of three-colour traffic lights featuring an arrow, the normal requirements for the indicated direction apply.


#### Responding to other road signs


To act in accordance with signs that convey a command or a prohibition.
You should also take note of other road signs, such as those that serve as warnings.
Every manned level crossing is fitted with an audible warning signal. In particular, in the event of atmospheric disturbances, thick fog and the like, this audible warning signal serves to draw the driver’s attention to the presence of a level crossing when a train is approaching.
Responding to the warning signal in good time means that you can slow down calmly. Even in situations where visibility is poor, the approaching driver can be warned of an approaching level crossing by means of a warning signal.


#### Giving/responding to signals


Brake lights and/or flashing warning lights on other vehicles require extra attention.


#### Slowing down, braking, stopping


If circumstances so require: brake in good time and stop if necessary.
Do not brake in such a way, unless necessary, that it causes or is likely to cause a danger or obstruction to other road users.
When braking, you should always be aware of the special nature of a level crossing.
Depending on the situation, braking too late or incorrectly may lead to situations that are undesirable or dangerous, particularly at level crossings.
Try to slow down gradually as much as possible to allow for traffic approaching from behind.


### Pedestrian crossing


A pedestrian crossing must be approached and crossed with care.
Pedestrians are extremely vulnerable in today’s traffic. That is why protective measures are essential. One such measure is the provision of crossing points for pedestrians and drivers of vehicles for people with disabilities.
The pedestrian crossing, which consists of so-called ‘zebra crossings’, is indicated by sign L2, often preceded by sign J22.
There are also crossings that are not designated as pedestrian crossings.
These areas are marked by road markings and may or may not be equipped with traffic lights. Drivers must also adapt their driving behaviour to this situation.
Pedestrians and drivers of vehicles for the disabled are free to use the crossing. However, the protective effect is only available when a pedestrian crossing is used.
If pedestrians or drivers of vehicles for the disabled are using a crossing where a flashing yellow light is in operation, they must give way to approaching drivers. However, those drivers must bear in mind that the pedestrian or driver of a vehicle for the disabled crossing the road assumes they are protected.


#### The interests of other road users


Approach a pedestrian crossing with caution.
This ensures that statutory requirements are properly met. It gives pedestrians and/or drivers of vehicles for the disabled who are using, or wish to use, that crossing the impression that their presence has been noticed and that they will be taken into account.
This kind of driving behaviour also alerts drivers approaching from behind to the presence of a pedestrian crossing, so that they too can adjust their driving in good time.
Certain manoeuvres are carried out with extra care at or near a pedestrian crossing. For example, a vehicle whose driver has stopped before a crossing for a reason other than to give way to pedestrians is only passed with great caution. Speed and visual behaviour are adjusted accordingly.


#### Viewing behaviour


Please take care when approaching.
In this way, in combination with the adjusted speed, the vehicle can be slowed down or brought to a halt in good time.
Careful attention must be paid to pedestrians and drivers of vehicles for the disabled who are a short distance from the pedestrian crossing. Their behaviour and the way they look around can often indicate whether they intend to cross there.


#### Give way to


Give way to pedestrians and drivers of vehicles for the disabled who are crossing at a pedestrian crossing or are clearly about to cross.
In any case, these two groups refer to: those who are waiting to cross the road.


#### Speed


A pedestrian crossing is being approached at a moderate speed.
The speed must be such that the vehicle can be brought to a stop in the normal manner under all circumstances.
It is important here that the way in which drivers approach road users who wish to cross gives them the impression that they will indeed be given the opportunity to cross safely.


#### Responding to traffic lights and instructions


Obey traffic lights at pedestrian crossings and other crossing points. The traffic warden’s stop sign must be obeyed.
Two-colour or three-colour traffic lights may be installed at (pedestrian) crossings. Two-colour traffic lights (amber/red) are usually installed at crossings that are used by pedestrians only occasionally.


#### Slowing down, braking, stopping


If circumstances so require, slow down in good time and stop if necessary.
Do not brake in such a way, unless absolutely necessary, that it causes or is likely to cause a danger or obstruction to other road users.
At a pedestrian crossing, it is very important to brake in the correct manner. This gives road users crossing the road the confidence that they can actually cross safely.
In particular, take road and weather conditions into account. This will allow traffic approaching from behind to react in good time. This is particularly important where pedestrians and/or drivers of vehicles for the disabled are crossing the road.


### Tram/bus stop


A tram/bus stop has been provided for passengers getting on and off.
The stop is marked with sign L3 (tram/bus stop). A tram/bus stop may be situated either on the right-hand side of the carriageway or on a traffic island in the middle of the carriageway.
In the latter case, passengers getting on or off must cross the carriageway to get to or from the pavement or the tram/bus. The hurried behaviour of passengers often leads to dangerous situations in such circumstances.


#### The interests of other road users


When driving past a tram or bus stop, particularly where there are large numbers of people waiting, be aware of (unexpected) situations that could pose a risk.
Adapt your driving style to the road conditions. Passengers getting on or off the vehicle often walk onto the carriageway without paying attention. Particularly when it is very busy and space is limited, children and elderly people can easily stray from the footpath or pavement. When driving past waiting passengers, do not drive through puddles (or only do so at a very low speed). This will help to prevent any inconvenience.
If there is one or more trams or buses at the stop, be aware that passengers may suddenly step onto the road in front of or behind a tram or bus.


#### Viewing behaviour


Take care when approaching or passing a tram or bus stop.
This applies in particular where there are narrower carriageways and/or smaller waiting areas for passengers. If a tram, bus or other vehicle is stationary at a stop, be mindful of passengers getting on or off. For various reasons, their attention is often focused on things other than the traffic.
This and other factors, such as rushing, etc., can lead to dangerous situations. You should also be mindful of trams or buses pulling away. Use your mirrors to check for traffic approaching from behind.


#### Give way to


When passing a stationary tram or bus, allow passengers the opportunity to get on or off.
If a bus driver indicates, using his indicator, that he wishes to turn off the road within a built-up area, give him the opportunity to do so.
Passengers getting on or off the bus must, if they are on the carriageway, be able to cross it unhindered. The bus driver must be given the opportunity to drive off. In doing so, account must be taken of the size of the bus and the space required for this vehicle.


#### Location on the road/scene of the incident


When driving past a tram or bus stop where people are waiting, leave plenty of space.


#### Speed


When approaching a tram/bus stop where a tram or bus is stationary and/or there are people waiting, adjust your speed.
As the gap between vehicles gets smaller, drive at a lower speed.


#### Giving/responding to signals


Respond appropriately to the bus’s indicator at a bus stop.
Often, the fact that the bus’s brake lights have come on is enough to suggest that the driver is about to drive off.


#### Slowing down, braking, stopping


If circumstances so require, brake in good time and stop if necessary.
Do not brake in such a way, unless absolutely necessary, that it causes or is likely to cause a danger or obstruction to other road users.
This also ensures that traffic approaching from behind is informed in good time.


### Roundabout


A roundabout is a junction of roads (as distinct from a crossroads) at which traffic flows in a circular pattern.
A roundabout is indicated by sign D1 in Annex 1 of the 1990 Road Traffic Regulations. On a roundabout, oncoming traffic is therefore, in theory, impossible. On roundabouts that are not indicated by sign D1, the statutory exceptions do not apply. In the past, roundabouts were used primarily at complex junctions (for example, those with more than four branches) to ensure a smoother flow of traffic. Nowadays, it is common practice to redesign junctions as mini-roundabouts. This is done to reduce speed, thereby improving safety at the location. At roundabouts, the layout and the expected behaviour are particularly different. It is precisely this aspect that makes a traditional roundabout a special section of road.


#### The interests of other road users


Do not cause any danger or more than necessary inconvenience to other road users.
This applies to all traffic, regardless of where it is or from which direction it is approaching.


#### Viewing behaviour


Take care when approaching, entering, driving through and leaving the roundabout.
At a roundabout, a large number of manoeuvres must be carried out. These will often involve other road users. It is therefore important that drivers remain constantly alert to changing traffic conditions.


##### Approaching


When approaching a roundabout, determine the following at the earliest possible stage:
− the nature of the roundabout (the presence of road signs, etc.)
− whether other vehicles are approaching or travelling on the roundabout
− whether there is any moped or bicycle traffic approaching from the right
− the general situation at and around the roundabout (type and condition of the road surface, visibility, special circumstances, etc.).
Check the mirrors to assess the situation behind the car. Just before entering the roundabout, look straight ahead and to the left to check whether it is safe to enter the roundabout. If sign B6 is present, adjust your line of sight even more carefully to the situation.


##### Driving onto the road


It is important to be aware of what is happening just in front of and, above all, behind the car. So: keep an eye on other road users by looking ahead and to the left, and check your mirrors to monitor the traffic behind you.
At sign B6 on the access road, drivers’ behaviour will be even more closely tailored to that situation.


##### At the roundabout


When changing lanes, check carefully to ensure you are not obstructing other drivers. In such situations in particular, it is important to check the area behind and to the side of your vehicle.
By looking in the rear-view mirror, the right-hand wing mirror and over your right shoulder, you can check that you are not being overtaken on the right at that moment.
By checking the rear-view mirror, the left-hand wing mirror and over your left shoulder, you can remain alert to the behaviour of drivers who may be intending to overtake on the left.
Looking behind you must not turn into looking back too often or for an unnecessarily long time. This can cause the car to veer off course. Moreover, you will then fail to see what is happening in front of the car.


##### On leaving


Keep an eye on any traffic to the right of the vehicle by checking the rear-view mirror, the right-hand wing mirror and over your right shoulder. Extra care is required on cycle lanes, cycle paths (including those that are separate from the road) and footpaths.


#### Right of way/giving way


When entering and whilst driving on a roundabout, the legal right of way must be observed.
When exiting the roundabout, give way to traffic continuing along the square. This includes traffic on a separate cycle path.


#### Location on the road/scene of the incident


The direction of travel and the volume of traffic determine which lane or position on the carriageway you should use. Do not obstruct cycle paths when approaching, using or leaving them.
This is the only way to ensure optimal traffic flow. Where possible, drive in the centre of the lane. As a general rule, do not use advisory lanes (without symbols) or cycle lanes (dashed lines). Different rules may apply when turning. These are set out in the section on junctions.


##### At roundabouts with three lanes


If these, like the access road, are not marked with lane divisions indicated by arrows:
At the quarter-lap mark
− move into the right-hand lane on the slip road in good time
− when approaching the roundabout, carry out the manoeuvres that must be performed before turning right
− from this position, enter, follow and exit the roundabout.
At the halfway point
− on the approach road, preferably use the central reservation
− drive onto the roundabout from that position
− as soon as you have passed the quarter-turn junction, move into the right-hand lane and carry out the manoeuvres required before turning right
− leave the roundabout.
At the three-quarter mark
− on the approach road, preferably use the left-hand lane
− drive onto the roundabout from that position
− stay in the left-hand lane on the roundabout
− as soon as you have passed the quarter-lap marker, move into the centre lane
− as soon as you have passed the half-turn junction, move into the right-hand lane and carry out the manoeuvres required before turning right
− then leave the roundabout.
When rounding off the total
− on the approach road, preferably use the left-hand lane
− drive onto the roundabout from that position
− stay in the left-hand lane on the roundabout
− as soon as you have passed the halfway point, move into the central lane
− as soon as you have passed the three-quarter-turn junction, move into the right-hand lane and then carry out the manoeuvres required before turning right
− then leave the roundabout.


##### At roundabouts with two lanes


The procedure here is the same as at three-lane roundabouts, except that:
− at quarter and half turns, ‘right-hand lane’ should be read as ‘centre lane’
− for three-quarters of a lap, drivers use the left-hand lane until they have passed the exit for the half-lap
− When completing a full lap, drivers must stay in the left-hand lane until they have passed the exit for three-quarters of a lap.
This procedure does not always apply:
− in asymmetrical squares
− at roundabouts without lanes
− at roundabouts with traffic lights and lanes marked with arrows
− in heavy traffic;
− for all types of vehicles in use.
If it is not possible to follow this procedure, adjust your driving style. Change lanes in a smooth, continuous motion.


#### Speed


Adjust your speed as you approach and whilst on the roundabout so that you can carry out all manoeuvres safely.
If sign B6 is present, adjust your speed as you approach and enter the roundabout to suit the situation even more carefully.


#### Responding to traffic lights and instructions


Obey the traffic lights for the chosen direction of travel.
At traffic lights where the amber light is flashing, extra caution is advised.


#### Responding to other road signs


Comply with road markings and/or other traffic signs that indicate a requirement or prohibition.
If the carriageway of the approach road to the roundabout is divided into lanes marked with arrows, move into the correct lane as early as possible. This depends in particular on the direction you are taking.
At the roundabout, too, always follow the lane where the arrows match your chosen
direction.


#### Giving/responding to signals


Signal in good time when changing lanes or leaving the roundabout.
The indicator is always deactivated as soon as the driver changes lanes or turns off the road.
If you are approaching the roundabout with the intention of taking the first exit, indicate right as you approach the roundabout. When you are three-quarters of the way round the roundabout, indicate left in good time whilst still on the approach road. Switch off your indicator as soon as you have entered the roundabout from your pre-selected position. To improve communication with other road users, it may be advisable – depending on factors such as the direction of travel – to keep the indicator on for longer (mini roundabouts). On roundabouts with more than one lane for the same direction, it may be advisable to signal differently. Otherwise, this could cause confusion amongst other road users.
When approaching and driving on the roundabout, it is very important to take into account the direction indicated by other drivers.


#### Slowing down, braking, stopping


If circumstances so require, brake in good time and stop if necessary.
Do not brake in such a way, unless absolutely necessary, that it causes or is likely to cause a danger or obstruction to other road users.
Always brake in a manner appropriate to the situation.


## 3.7 Special manoeuvres


The following actions/manoeuvres are classified as special manoeuvres:
− Getting on and off
− Preparatory and control procedures
− Reversing in a straight line
− Reversing in a curve
− Parking in a perpendicular or diagonal bay
− Parallel parking
− Turning around by crossing
− Turning around by a half turn
− Hill start
Every driver will find in practice that they regularly have to carry out special manoeuvres. For example, a driver may find themselves in a cul-de-sac, meaning they will have to turn the car round. Parking, too, is something drivers do almost every day. It is clear that drivers need to have certain skills.
Instructions and performance during the test The candidate is given a specific instruction to carry out a special manoeuvre on a street or in a car park. For example: “On this street, you are to turn around by crossing the road.” The candidate then chooses a location where to carry out this manoeuvre.
During the test, the candidate is personally responsible for maintaining good control of the vehicle, driving with a certain degree of fluency, paying close attention to other road users, choosing a safe location and driving in an environmentally conscious manner.


### Viewing behaviour


One’s field of vision must be adjusted in such a way that one is confident the manoeuvre can be carried out safely. Safety must also be maintained throughout the manoeuvre. To this end, one should make use of the mirrors provided and look over one’s shoulder. In addition, assistance systems, such as a reversing camera, may be used.


### Vehicle control


Good vehicle control means correct use of the accelerator and clutch pedals and the service and/or parking brake. This must be combined with good and timely steering, taking into account the size of one’s own vehicle. Furthermore, the manoeuvre should be carried out as smoothly as possible, without stopping unless absolutely necessary.


### Adaptable and decisive


Special manoeuvres must not be carried out so slowly that other traffic is, or could be, held up for longer than is necessary.


### The interests of other road users


Before and during the execution of any special manoeuvre, one must ensure that the manoeuvre can be carried out without causing more than the necessary disruption to other traffic.


### Give way to


When carrying out a special manoeuvre, give way to other traffic.
This applies to all road users, regardless of the direction from which they are approaching.


### Location on the road / scene of the incident


The operation is carried out in a place where there is sufficient space and a clear view.
Take environmental considerations into account as much as possible. Every effort should be made to minimise the time spent with the engine idling.


### Getting on and off


Getting on and off are regarded together as a single special manoeuvre.
The aim is to:
− to get in and out safely
− not to leave car doors open for longer than is necessary
When getting in and out of a vehicle, opening and leaving a door open is an important consideration. This is because it can cause a nuisance or even pose a danger to others. # Application




## Introduction to the application


When assessing the candidate, the examiner must be fully aware that the candidate makes their own choices and applies their own preferences. As a result, the candidate may demonstrate a different solution to a road traffic task than that expected by the examiner, or than the examiner’s preferred solution. However, this does not mean that the candidate’s solution is necessarily incorrect or unsafe. Each aspect of road use is defined by means of assessment criteria (actions).
Candidates are permitted to use driver assistance systems during driving tests for passenger cars. The examiner assesses the candidate’s driving ability on the basis of their driving procedure, which may involve the use of driver assistance systems or not. The examiner does not assess the decision to use or not to use driver assistance systems. There are two exceptions to the use of driver assistance systems:
• The automatic parking system must not be used when performing the special manoeuvres during theory and practical tests. The reason for this is that, during the special manoeuvres section, the examiner assesses the candidate’s ability to operate and control the vehicle within a confined space.
• ‘Hands-off, eyes-on’ systems must not be used1. The reason for this is that the examiner cannot carry out a full assessment of a candidate’s driving ability if these ‘hands-off, eyes-on’ driver assistance systems are activated. It is expected that tasks requiring assessment of the candidate’s driving ability will be removed, to be replaced by monitoring and override tasks.
The Driving Procedure can be applied in full throughout the training programme. However, it would be going too far to include all the aspects mentioned in the test. The examiner will therefore need to distinguish between what is more relevant to the training programme and what forms part of the assessment.
In practical driving tests, examiners are confronted with behaviour that deviates from the Driving Procedure. Such behaviour is identified on the basis of what is set out in that Driving Procedure. However, the Driving Procedure does not specify how, from an assessment perspective, such deviant behaviour should be dealt with. This application addresses that issue.
This document is a guideline intended for examiners. It primarily sets out the essential assessment criteria. However, to ensure a consistent interpretation, it also includes a number of non-essential assessment criteria.
When assessing a particular component, all relevant actions are taken into account, including the provisions set out in Chapters 1 and 2. In this application, the essential topics for each component are specified.
The extent to which actions that have not been carried out, or have not been carried out properly, are taken into account in the assessment depends on one of the following AEX factors:
A: the nature. The term ‘nature’ refers to the specific act or omission.
E. Severity: Severity refers to the extent to which the performance of an act (or the failure to fulfil a particular obligation) deviates from the prescribed conduct.
X the number of times. When considering the number of times, it is important to bear in mind whether the relevant task, in relation to the component, is likely to feature frequently or less frequently in the exam.
1 The term ‘hands-off, eyes-on’ systems refers to systems where you must keep your eyes on the road (and are responsible for doing so) and where you are allowed to take your hands off the steering wheel.
When assessing behaviour, any additional factors such as road, weather and traffic conditions are also taken into account. It is therefore possible that behaviour which would normally be considered unusual may nevertheless be deemed acceptable in certain circumstances.
When assessing driving ability, the overall picture of the test is important. The key question is whether it is safe to allow the candidate to drive independently in traffic. The examiner will take this into account explicitly in their final assessment.
Consequently, it is possible that, even though one or perhaps even several elements have not been performed correctly, the candidate’s overall performance is such that they can still be deemed fit to drive.
However, if all relevant aspects are taken into account in the final assessment and the examiner deems one or more components to be unsatisfactory, this also means that the final result of the examination is unsatisfactory. The candidate has then failed.
In that case, the list of areas requiring improvement will specify which assessment criteria gave rise to this. The driving test procedure must therefore be ‘translated’ into the final result.


## Scope of Chapter 1


The following topics – clutch, gear mechanism, throttle control and brakes – are not essential. An exception to this is where the operation has, or could have, a significant impact on road safety. In that case, the operation may be classified as essential.
Chapter 1 is of a general nature. This means that the topics described therein relate to all the examination components included in Chapter 3. Preparing the vehicle for the road and operating it form the basis of the ability to participate correctly in traffic. This basic behaviour is given significant weight in the assessment, and any deviation from it in this respect is taken into account directly in the assessment. A candidate who consistently displays deviant behaviour in their basic conduct will not be able to pass the test. This may involve regularly displaying deviant behaviour in a single test component or the same deviation across multiple test components. To determine whether this deviant behaviour should also be regarded as culpable, the principles set out in the general text remain the starting point.
In addition to the preparatory and checking procedures (Chapter 3), the basic behaviour described in Chapter 1 makes it possible to assess driving with, for example, an incorrectly adjusted seat and/or mirrors.
Preparing the vehicle for the road and its operation/control are assessed as a single, integrated whole under the heading ‘vehicle handling’. This emphasises the importance of good basic driving behaviour.


### Clutch operation


The following applies:
− it is essential to apply the clutch pedal with the correct amount of pressure
− particularly when pulling away, raising the clutch pedal too quickly or not quickly enough can be a nuisance
− driving with the clutch fully or partially depressed unnecessarily, or for longer than is necessary, must be avoided
− driving with the engine disengaged when cornering can be dangerous.


### Operation of the switching mechanism


In fact, it is the engine speed, in relation to the vehicle speed, that is the determining factor. Newer engines have a greater tolerance than older ones. Incorrect gear changes occur when:
− failing to change to a higher or lower gear in good time
− the gear is shifted into the wrong one
− the candidate is unable to select the correct gear.


### Gas supply control


The following applies:
− proper control of the accelerator pedal, particularly when driving at low speeds, is essential for the correct regulation of the fuel supply
− if you press the accelerator unevenly, the car will drive in fits and starts
− the engine may ‘surge’ if, when the engine is disengaged, more throttle is applied than is necessary.


### Brake operation


This concerns the technical operation of the vehicle. Failing to brake in a particular situation, or braking too late, is not related to this.
In such cases, the topics covered include, for example, slowing down, braking, stopping or giving way (Chapter 3). The term ‘brake’ refers to both the service brake and the parking brake.
With regard to the operation of the brake, the following applies:
− when engaged, the system applies the brakes and only disengages when the engine speed requires it
− that braking unnecessarily abruptly is incorrect
− on a slippery road surface, do not brake in such a way as to cause the tyres to skid.


### Controls for lighting and other (auxiliary) equipment


Various aspects may be relevant here. For example:
− the required lights are switched on when setting off
− the (correct) lights are switched on whilst driving, if conditions change to such an extent that lighting is required
− if necessary, the main beam is dimmed
− the horn is used when necessary
− the windscreen wipers switch on when it rains
− when the rear window fogs up, the rear window heater is switched on
− when the windows mist up, the demisting function is switched on, possibly in conjunction with the air conditioning.
It also happens that, although the need to switch on the lights is recognised, the relevant switch cannot be found or is operated incorrectly.
The candidate must be able to operate this equipment on their own initiative and without the assistance of third parties. It is important that the candidate keeps their attention on driving the vehicle. If this is not the case, it can quickly lead to other culpable behaviour.


### Vehicle control


Vehicle control is a key area of assessment. Insufficient vehicle control is evident in candidates who:
− is completely unable to control the vehicle whilst driving
− lacks sufficient directional stability.
Total inability to drive is not limited to candidates who make mistakes when operating the clutch, gear lever, accelerator or brakes. It refers to a candidate who, as a result of such poor overall control, does not have ‘mastery’ over the vehicle. Insufficient control of the vehicle is only considered an issue if the candidate displays this behaviour in more than one test component, or if it occurs frequently within the same component. This does not apply to the ‘special manoeuvres’ component. Such poor control will be evident in a variety of situations (i.e. across different examination components). The consequence is often that other, non-vehicle-related actions are also carried out incorrectly. In relation to the examination components, this also leads to other culpable behaviour.
In the event of insufficient vehicle control, in addition to the ‘operation/control’ assessment item, the assessment items that were incorrectly performed as a result will also be noted.
Insufficient steering control will become apparent during the test through a number of poorly executed ‘steering manoeuvres’. The candidate struggles to maintain the correct position on the carriageway in a variety of situations, and manoeuvres are often not carried out in a smooth, continuous line. Furthermore, a candidate lacking steering control may, for example, cause the car to veer off course whilst checking their mirrors or looking over their shoulder, and/or whilst carrying out (technical) manoeuvres. In the event of insufficient steering control, in addition to the candidate’s operation and control of the vehicle, the assessment criteria that were incorrectly performed as a result are also noted.
Lack of control over the vehicle is only considered to be the case when this behaviour is of a systematic nature. In other words: this behaviour must occur in several sections or frequently within the same section in order to be deemed culpable.


### Environmentally conscious driving


The assessment of this assessment criterion will be taken into account in the final assessment. If the environmentally conscious driving behaviour deviates from the most desirable standard, the examiner will assess this deviation using the current AEX grading system. This will be weighed against performance in the areas of safety and traffic flow. Deviations in the assessment criterion ‘Environmentally conscious driving’ may be decisive for the outcome of the entire test.
If the candidate shows absolutely no deviant behaviour in terms of safety and traffic flow, then deviant behaviour relating solely to environmentally conscious driving cannot, on its own, result in a fail.


## Application: Chapter 2


The topics covered here are also of a general nature. They concern ‘appropriate and decisive behaviour’ and ‘the interests of other road users’. Behaviour falling under these topics must be a recurring pattern in order to be regarded as culpable. The topics ‘appropriate and decisive behaviour’ and ‘the interests of other road users’ are essential.
Traffic awareness is not directly taken into account in the assessment. In practice, a lack of such awareness will often automatically lead to specific, culpable behaviour. That behaviour then falls under the relevant heading.


### Appropriate and decisive behaviour


This involves two different leadership characteristics: adaptable and decisive behaviour.


#### Changed behaviour


Adaptive driving means driving at a speed that is as closely as possible suited to other traffic and the prevailing conditions. It is impossible to express this in kilometres per hour. After all, traffic conditions are constantly changing. Speed must always be adjusted to the current traffic and/or road conditions. Even when there is no (or very little) traffic, driving at a speed that does not correspond to what is customary in that situation constitutes behaviour that is not sufficiently adapted. Furthermore, the candidate must demonstrate a certain fluidity when carrying out the required manoeuvres. In this context, however, ‘fluid’ is not to be interpreted as ‘fast’ or ‘hasty’. A candidate who drives in an inappropriate manner often not only moves too slowly through traffic, but also performs most manoeuvres (looking, changing gear, braking, etc.) too slowly. During the test, candidates who do not drive appropriately are made aware of their incorrect behaviour so that they can correct it. Appropriate behaviour does not refer to candidates who drive at excessive speeds. Such behaviour falls under the topic of speed.


#### Decisive behaviour


The candidate must demonstrate a certain degree of decisiveness. Their overall behaviour must reflect a degree of confidence aimed at positively influencing road safety and the flow of traffic.
The indecisive candidate regularly hesitates over whether or not certain actions should be carried out. Consequently, a manoeuvre is often initiated at the wrong moment. Such behaviour will therefore frequently lead to other behaviour that is clearly attributable to the candidate. The candidate’s overall driving performance must convince the examiner that they lack decisiveness.
Given its systemic nature, this behaviour will need to occur across several departments or frequently within the same department in order to be deemed culpable.
If the candidate’s behaviour is deemed insufficiently appropriate or decisive, this will result in a fail. In addition to inappropriate or indecisive behaviour, any related issues that arise as a result may also be noted.


### The interests of other road users


This topic is covered both in Chapter 2 and in each of the sections described in Chapter 3. Chapter 2 deals with socially responsible driving behaviour. Chapter 3 focuses primarily on behaviour on the road that is not regulated, or not fully regulated.
The interests of other road users are a key consideration.
The candidate must take it for granted that, in addition to obeying the rules of the road, considerate driving is essential for road safety.
Without detracting from qualities such as adaptability and decisiveness, their behaviour must demonstrate a willingness to take account of the interests of other road users. For example,
Where safety permits, one should always respond to mistakes made by others.
The section on ‘The interests of other road users’ in Chapter 3 is more closely linked to the general provision of Article 5 of the Road Traffic Act.
This section sets out the principles of safety and traffic flow. For example, the assessment criterion ‘Interests of other road users’ from Chapter 2 will be of a structural nature, in contrast to the ‘Interests’ from Chapter 3.


## Application: Chapter 3


Unlike Chapters 1 and 2, the manoeuvres included in this chapter relate solely to the examination component in which they are included. Various examination components consist of several or different manoeuvres. Taken together, these then form the basis for the component to be assessed.
With regard to all examination components included in this chapter, the following principles apply in relation to the assessment criteria set out below:
− The introductory text at the start of each section is intended to set out certain facts and basic principles. This helps to define the scope of the section
− The interests of other road users are only relevant if certain (culpable) behaviour is not explicitly described under another assessment criterion. The interests of other road users are an essential assessment criterion
− Observation behaviour is only taken into account in the assessment if there is a direct link to the obligation to give way. In other cases, observation behaviour is only considered for inclusion in the assessment if there is a direct link to safety. In order to classify the required visual behaviour as inadequate, it must be absolutely certain that the candidate failed to look, looked insufficiently or looked incorrectly.
− Speed is only taken into account in the assessment if the driver is travelling at excessive speed, or if they are merging, overtaking or changing lanes at too low a speed
− Responding to other road signs refers only to those signs that impose a requirement or a prohibition. Ignoring other types of signs automatically constitutes a different form of culpable (and therefore assessable) behaviour
− ‘Position on the road’ refers to the specific position to be occupied on the road.
− The location of an action refers to the place where an action (e.g. overtaking) is carried out or commenced.
− Slowing down, braking and stopping have a more general context. It is applied, amongst other things, where the candidate fails to slow down or stop in good time in accordance with the general obligation – also set out in Article 19 of the 1990 Road Traffic Regulations – to come to a halt in good time and, for example, to compensate for an error made by another road user. It does not, therefore, apply if, for example, the candidate fails to give way or to comply with road signs.
Within each examination component, certain actions (assessment criteria) are essential for the examination to be conducted properly and safely. These are classified as essential. The other criteria, which are not classified as such, are still taken into account in the assessment but (normally) carry less weight.


### 3.1 Setting off


The following are essential:
− the interests of other road users
− viewing behaviour
− let them go first.
When assessing this section, only pulling away after stopping outside the flow of traffic is taken into account. When joining the carriageway from a car park, the procedure to follow is the same as that for pulling away from an exit.


#### Viewing behaviour


A distinction is made between failing to look, looking insufficiently, or looking incorrectly. Failing to look will generally be regarded as more culpable behaviour. Insufficient looking occurs, for example, when the driver does look correctly but does not set off immediately afterwards. Incorrect observation occurs when, although the driver is observing in accordance with the driving procedure, the observation is not functional. The ability to properly observe any traffic approaching from behind depends, amongst other things, on road and weather conditions and/or traffic density. Depending on the circumstances, this may give rise to certain nuances in the assessment. If the candidate’s checking behaviour is such that they could have seen that no other traffic is approaching, checking whilst pulling away is no longer strictly required.


#### Give way to


Give way to other traffic.


##### Pulling away after stopping in traffic




#### The interests of other road users


Pulling away, such as when accelerating from a traffic light or after giving way, is not taken into account in the assessment. This does not apply if the candidate’s behaviour (or negligence) results in an unsafe situation. If, after coming to a stop in traffic, the candidate comes into conflict or is at risk of coming into conflict with other road users (cyclists/moped riders), the ‘interests of other road users’ are at stake.


### 3.2 Driving on straight and winding sections of road


The following are essential:
− the interests of other road users
− viewing behaviour
− right of way/giving way
− location on the road/place where the incident occurred
− keep a safe following distance
− speed
− slow down, brake, stop.
It is important to determine which assessment criteria apply to a particular type of behaviour. This is described under the various assessment criteria. For example, the following distance may not apply when meeting oncoming traffic or when overtaking, nor does the driver’s gaze behaviour apply when driving on straight sections of road.


#### Viewing behaviour


Culpable visual behaviour may be an issue, particularly when swerving. Paying close attention is especially important during more significant lateral movements. Visual behaviour whilst driving on straight and winding sections of road will not normally be taken into account in the assessment.


#### To give way/let someone go first


In exceptional circumstances, it may be necessary to give way on straight and bended sections of road. For example, in the case of emergency vehicles and blind people.


#### Location on the road/scene of the incident


Occasional failure to maintain the correct position on the carriageway has little impact on the assessment, unless a dangerous situation arises. However, this is not the case if the candidate regularly appears to have difficulty choosing or maintaining the correct position.
For the purposes of the assessment, it is also important to consider whether or not this is a result of the candidate’s steering stability. If that is the case, other steering manoeuvres will often be carried out incorrectly as well. In such a situation, the issue of vehicle control is also at stake.
‘Keep as far to the right as possible’ means: in accordance with the road conditions and any other circumstances. All circumstances are therefore taken into account when assessing this behaviour. Incorrect bends include, amongst others, bends that are ‘cut short’ or taken too wide. Unnecessary ‘oversteering’, most commonly seen on right-hand bends, is also incorrect. If the candidate regularly struggles to transition smoothly from a curved line to a straight one, and this line is only achieved by swerving or making corrective manoeuvres, this constitutes a lack of steering control.
Failure to give way, or giving way insufficiently or too late, when meeting oncoming traffic – particularly outside built-up areas – can quickly lead to dangerous situations. Giving way will mainly be required when meeting other drivers. When being overtaken, the overtaking driver is the one who takes the initiative. The learner driver may only be required to give way in certain situations or under special circumstances.


#### Following distance


This may involve various aspects. When following a vehicle over a certain distance, the safe following distance is important. The candidate therefore has no intention of overtaking.
Particularly outside built-up areas, you must be aware of any drivers approaching from behind who may wish to merge into traffic after overtaking. Even when following another vehicle at a shorter distance, you must maintain a sufficient distance. You must always be able to bring your own vehicle to a safe stop in good time. An important factor in the assessment is the frequency with which such behaviour occurs. Failing to maintain a safe distance on a single occasion does not necessarily have to be regarded as serious. However, it is considered serious if the situation creates a hazard or if such behaviour occurs more frequently.


#### Speed


If the candidate is driving too fast, they should be made aware of this. Bends in the road can appear unexpectedly quickly. It is precisely candidates with little experience who will find it difficult, if not impossible, to keep the car under control. Recognise this in good time and take corrective action!
Speed can be particularly important when being overtaken. In such situations, you must not increase your speed. This can lead to dangerous situations, particularly on single-lane roads – including within built-up areas – especially when there is oncoming traffic. By not increasing their speed, the driver being overtaken helps to ensure that the overtaking manoeuvre – at least as far as their presence is concerned – does not take longer than necessary.


#### Slowing down, braking, stopping


Failing to brake, or braking too late, on the odd occasion need not have a significant impact on the assessment (unless it creates a hazard).
Unnecessary braking constitutes improper behaviour. Furthermore, if this is done in such a way that it causes, or is likely to cause, danger or obstruction, it constitutes culpable behaviour. Some driving test candidates are quick to stop. Particularly when conditions are not ideal for doing so, other drivers (approaching from behind) will not expect this. This can quickly lead to dangerous situations. This is particularly relevant in exceptional circumstances when meeting oncoming traffic. The candidate must therefore anticipate the situation properly by braking or stopping. Failure to do so may lead to undesirable situations.


### 3.3 Behaviour near and at junctions


The following are essential:
− the interests of other road users
− viewing behaviour
− right of way/giving way
− speed
− responding to traffic lights/signs.
The distinction between giving way and allowing others to proceed remains fully in force in this context. The definition of ‘giving way’ (1.am of the 1990 Road Traffic Regulations) ensures that there is a difference in the scope of the two concepts. For instance, in the case of ‘allowing another vehicle to pass’, where other traffic is present (Article 18 of the 1990 Road Traffic Regulations), the assessment criterion of ‘the interests of other road users’ may also be relevant in the event of unnecessary obstruction of that traffic.
When turning off, the position on the road or the location of the manoeuvre may also be important, particularly if this is a recurring factor when making turns.
When entering a driveway, the same standards generally apply. As turning also takes place at junctions, both the manoeuvres involved in turning and those relating to behaviour at junctions must be carried out correctly.


#### Viewing behaviour


Deviating observation behaviour will need to occur on several occasions before it can be regarded as culpable. A single instance of failing to pay attention, or paying insufficient attention, will almost never directly result in culpable observation behaviour. At signs B6 or B7, the driver’s behaviour must be more closely attuned to that junction. After all, the different right-of-way rule requires this. Failing to pay proper attention at these signs has a significant impact on the assessment of this matter.
When turning off, there are various ways in which one’s gaze can be incorrect. For example, one may fail to look, look in a way other than that prescribed, or look at the wrong moment. The manner in which one looks, the frequency with which one looks, and the order in which one looks are all important factors in this regard.
A key factor in the assessment is the number of times the driver has failed to comply. When turning right, it is essential to look over the right shoulder. However, this requirement need not be applied so strictly in all circumstances. The manner in which one must look also depends in part on the road and traffic conditions. All these factors must be recognised and taken into account in the assessment.


#### Right of way/giving way


The manner of approach is a contributing factor here. The obligation to give way applies only to drivers. If a pedestrian crosses a junction without the candidate necessarily reacting to this, the topics of slowing down, braking and stopping come into play. Giving way may be relevant in relation to the obligation towards emergency vehicles, trams turning off, blind people and the like.
If, at the same time as the candidate, other drivers are approaching that junction via other roads, any ‘opportunity to proceed’ may be taken, provided this is done in a responsible manner. This also applies if a driver with right of way slows down or stops entirely of their own accord.
Although giving way unnecessarily – particularly when this occurs frequently – is a breach of the rules, such behaviour does not fall within the scope of this topic. After all, there is no breach of the right-of-way rule as such. In such cases, the matter is dealt with under the relevant section or resolution.
When turning off, you must give way to traffic that has the right of way. However, if that traffic stops of its own accord, without being forced to do so, as it were, then you may take advantage of the opportunity presented.
Giving way to other traffic may already be an issue when merging into a lane. However, it is important to realise that, depending on the road and/or traffic conditions, the
it is not always possible to prevent some disruption.


#### Location on the road/scene of the incident


The correct position on the road is particularly important when negotiating bends. The extent to which a driver deviates from the correct position and the frequency with which this behaviour occurs are key factors in the assessment. Incorrect lane positioning is normally less significant. However, it becomes more important if, when turning left on a two-way road, the driver veers entirely or excessively to the left. In that case, the driver must correct their course in good time. If the candidate is unable to transition smoothly from bends into straight sections, but can only manage this by swerving or making corrective manoeuvres, this indicates a lack of steering control.


#### Speed


A number of factors are decisive. For example, when approaching a junction, speed must be adjusted to suit the road and traffic conditions. Factors such as visibility, weather and so on are of great importance in this regard. When assessing (in particular) culpable speed, the speed itself and/or the number of times a junction is approached or entered at excessive speed are key factors. Where sign B6 or B7 is present, speed must be explicitly adjusted accordingly. If the candidate fails to do so, this will again have a significant impact on the assessment.
Driving at excessive speed can be particularly dangerous on bends. In such cases, correct your course in good time. In the practical test, the subject of ‘speed’ remains a key consideration. Taking a bend at an inappropriate speed is likely to occur more frequently. This behaviour falls under the category of ‘adaptive/decisive’, as is normally the case when accelerating too late or too slowly after turning off.


#### Responding to traffic lights and instructions


When deciding whether or not to stop at a yellow light, certain factors may come into play. For example, the speed at which the candidate is travelling and the distance they are from the traffic light when it turns yellow are important. Sometimes it is preferable to carry on driving, particularly when there is ‘heavy’ traffic immediately behind the test car. However, candidates are often inclined to brake sharply. It is important to bear this in mind.
If a candidate fails to stop at a yellow traffic light, the circumstances prevailing at the time will be taken into account in the assessment.


#### Giving/responding to signals


Indicating ‘in good time’ depends on a number of factors. These may include, amongst other things, the volume of traffic, the road being used, the distance between junctions and the speed at which you are travelling.


### 3.4 Insert – Remove


The following are essential:
− the interests of other road users
− viewing behaviour
− let go first
− location on the road/scene of the incident.
− speed.


#### Viewing behaviour


In such cases, looking over the shoulder is mandatory. However, if the candidate fails to do so, whilst having clearly established at an earlier stage that no other traffic was approaching, this deviation may be dealt with in a nuanced manner. Similarly, when entering a combined merging and exit lane without actually merging, it is not always necessary to look over one’s shoulder in all circumstances. Here too, the driver’s prior observation behaviour is important.
Furthermore, there is no need to explicitly require drivers to look over their shoulder if, when merging, there is no paved section of road adjacent to the through carriageway and the driver has entered the exit lane at the correct point.


#### Give way to


When merging into or exiting traffic, drivers must give way to other traffic. Where two different drivers are merging into or exiting traffic at the same time, failure to give way is considered to be the fault of the driver merging into the traffic only.


#### Location on the road/scene of the incident


If, due to heavy traffic on the main carriageway, you need to stop on, for example, a short ‘dead-end’ merging lane, you should preferably stop at the start of that lane. If, in such a situation, you stop at the end of the lane, you will no longer be able to merge at the correct speed.
This also means that drivers will have to wait (even) longer for an opportunity to merge, with all the associated risks. If traffic travelling in the right-hand lane of the through carriageway moves of its own accord into a lane further to the left, the candidate may take advantage of the opportunity to merge. If, whilst driving on a long merging or exit lane comprising multiple lanes, the driver changes lanes, the ‘changing lanes’ section applies in the event of deviant behaviour.


#### Following distance


Maintaining the prescribed following distance is also an important consideration both before and whilst merging. However, in cases where there is heavy traffic on the main carriageway, it may be necessary – in order to merge safely – to temporarily fall short of the ‘two-second rule’ with regard to the traffic on the main carriageway. If the prescribed following distance is then gradually increased again, this cannot be regarded as culpable behaviour.


#### Speed


If a driver merges into the through lane at a speed that differs so significantly from that of the traffic on the through lane that it causes difficulties for other road users, this behaviour may be regarded as seriously culpable. If a vehicle merges at a time when there is no traffic approaching from behind on the through lane, driving at a slightly lower speed than usual may be permitted. However, this is subject to the condition that the speed is increased appropriately immediately afterwards.


### 3.5 Overtaking / moving sideways


The following are essential:
− the interests of other road users
− viewing behaviour
− let go first
− location on the road/scene of the incident.
The assessment criterion ‘Speed’ is not essential. However, it is considered important when changing lanes. In particular, the candidate must ensure that their speed is adjusted before beginning to change lanes. When overtaking, the consequences of an incorrectly adjusted speed are also assessed. This may then be noted under the assessment criteria ‘Giving way’ or ‘Consideration for other road users’.
Regardless of the road and/or traffic conditions, a lane change is required if a lateral movement is carried out without turning off, overtaking or merging into or out of traffic.
When turning off, it may be the case that both the carriageway of the road being left and that of the road being joined consist of several lanes. If, during this manoeuvre, the driver fails to remain in their own (originally occupied) lane and this involves culpable behaviour, the offence of overtaking or changing lanes applies.
The 1990 Road Traffic Regulations (RVV 1990) refer, in relation to giving way to other traffic, to the situation where a change of lane is made. The Driving Procedure goes further and also covers lateral movements that do not result in a change of lane. After all, not all carriageways are divided into lanes, whilst the consequences of an incorrect manoeuvre can be the same.
Overtaking and changing lanes, as well as moving sideways, are covered in this section. Both manoeuvres are very similar and relate to the same basic type of behaviour.


#### Viewing behaviour


Once again, deviant behaviour is assessed taking into account any additional factors. For example, the extent to which a driver swerves, depending on road and/or traffic conditions, plays a role. The circumstances under which such behaviour occurs and the frequency with which it takes place are also important. Furthermore, in the case of a candidate who regularly checks their mirrors and demonstrates an awareness of the possible presence of other traffic, deviating behaviour may be regarded as not, or less, culpable.


#### Give way to


As regards overtaking, this topic does not refer to any explicit legal obligation. Nevertheless, it should be regarded as standard practice that, when overtaking or passing and entering the section of the road intended for oncoming traffic, that traffic should be given way. Drivers approaching from behind who are overtaking the candidate should also be given way.
When overtaking, in particular, it is important to assess the distance to any oncoming drivers and the speed at which they are approaching. Overtaking can be risky, especially on roads where vehicles travel at higher speeds. Although the candidate may also encounter oncoming traffic whilst passing an obstacle, the fact that the obstacle is stationary makes it easier to make this assessment.
When changing lanes or moving sideways, regardless of the extent of that movement, you are obliged to give way to other road users. Your observation behaviour must also be adapted accordingly.
In practice, candidates will often have to make a lateral manoeuvre without having been given a specific instruction to do so, for example as a result of changing road or traffic conditions. Consequently, in many cases, the candidate’s ability to assess the traffic situation will also be put to the test.


#### Location on the road/scene of the incident


Overtaking other road users, particularly those on two wheels, whilst leaving insufficient space is quickly dangerous. In this regard, extra care must be taken to account for the greater vulnerability of, amongst others, children, the elderly and cyclists. Additional factors may also apply when overtaking in places where it is better to refrain from doing so. In this context, factors such as carriageway width, visibility and weather conditions are important. If – before swerving – the prescribed ‘following distance’ is not observed, this falls under the category of ‘position on the road’. Potentially dangerous situations can be prevented by taking corrective action in good time. When passing obstacles, one may be confronted, for example, with children who suddenly run onto the carriageway from between those obstacles. Passing obstacles with (too) little space between the vehicle and them is therefore a dangerous manoeuvre.


#### Speed


Before changing lanes, you must adjust your speed to suit the manoeuvre.


#### Giving/responding to signals


Unlike the practice of checking your surroundings before every manoeuvre, signalling is not compulsory for all manoeuvres. This applies only to significant manoeuvres, regardless of whether a lane change is involved. Signalling in cases other than those prescribed should be kept to a minimum.


### 3.6 Behaviour near and on specific sections of road


In the section on special road sections, ‘Interests of other road users’ is essential. For each special road section, the other assessment criteria that are essential will be specified.
The list of special road sections set out in the Driving Test Procedure is not exhaustive. It is possible that, during the test, a different type of road section may be classified as a special road section. Such a section must, however, meet the criteria set out in the Driving Procedure and must therefore differ clearly from other road sections in terms of function and/or layout. These are therefore locations where, unlike, for example, a speed bump where the driver merely needs to reduce speed, a distinctly different driving behaviour is required of the driver.


#### Entrance/exit


Exiting a junction, which requires compliance with Article 54 of the 1990 Road Traffic Regulations, falls under this section. Before entering the slip road, you must first complete your manoeuvre at the junction. Actions relating to turning off and junctions should be assessed under the ‘junctions’ section.
The following are essential:
− viewing behaviour
− let go first
− speed.


#### Viewing behaviour


It is very important to adapt your vision to the circumstances. You will need to pay extra attention to any pedestrians and the like when crossing the pavement.


#### Give way to


As a special manoeuvre is being carried out here, all other traffic must give way. Where vehicles are entering or exiting the slip road at the same time, both drivers have the same obligation.
Failure to ‘give way’ will then be the sole responsibility of the driver entering the junction. Should the driver leaving the junction fail to react correctly, the issue of slowing down, braking and stopping comes into play.


#### Speed


Speed is important, depending on the extent to which and the circumstances under which it is applied.


##### Yard


When riding on a farmyard, all actions carried out are assessed under this section. In this context, riding behaviour must be adapted to the farmyard’s specific purpose and, in particular, to the situations one may encounter (children playing, etc.).
The following are essential:
− viewing behaviour
− right of way / give way
− speed.


#### Viewing behaviour


Incorrect viewing behaviour is identified on the basis of the descriptions set out in the relevant sections. The same nuances are applied during the assessment.


#### Right of way/giving way


The right-of-way rules within the farmyard are no different from the usual ones and should also be properly observed by the candidate.


##### Crossing


The following are essential:
− viewing behaviour
− right of way / give way
− speed
− responding to traffic lights / instructions.


#### Viewing behaviour


It is particularly important to look carefully at level crossings fitted solely with an automatic warning light system (AKI). That said, you must still look carefully at other types of level crossings as well. Malfunctions in the equipment and similar issues can, in particular, lead to dangerous situations.


#### Location on the road/scene of the incident


Blocking level crossings is dangerous.


#### Speed


The factors outlined under ‘viewing behaviour’ also apply here. For example, a spacious, clearly laid-out and well-secured level crossing can be approached at a different speed to one that is equipped only with an AKI, where visibility is also poor.


#### Responding to traffic lights and instructions


A level crossing protected by an AHOB system or by other means should only be approached when the red flashing light has gone out. However, in such situations, a candidate will often be inclined to adapt their own driving behaviour to that of other road users.


##### Pedestrian crossing


The following are essential:
− let go first
− speed.
Naturally, good observation is the basis for correct behaviour, but this applies to all actions carried out in traffic. Observation as such – barring extreme deviations – is not taken into account in the assessment.


#### Give way to


This obligation must be interpreted broadly. It must be possible to cross the road safely and unhindered without having to adjust one’s pace in the slightest. However, this does not mean that, for the sake of giving way, one must stop for, for example, a pedestrian who is on the other side of the carriageway and therefore still a considerable distance away from the candidate.


#### Speed


This is adapted to the situation on the ground. This means that in a spacious, clear situation with no pedestrians crossing, you can drive at a different speed than when the (road/traffic) situation is different or when pedestrians are (about to) cross.


##### Tram/bus stop


The following are essential:
− let go first
− speed.


#### Speed


This must be adapted to the situation on the ground. Road and/or traffic conditions, such as people waiting, a tram or bus stationary at a stop, and so on, are factors that have an impact. If there are no buses, trams or people waiting, the normal speed may be maintained.


##### Roundabout


When approaching, driving through and leaving a roundabout, all manoeuvres carried out are assessed under this section.
The following are essential:
− viewing behaviour
− give way and let someone go first
− location on the road/place where the incident occurred
− responding to traffic lights/signs.


#### Viewing behaviour


All visual behaviour observed at the roundabout, regardless of its purpose or the situation in which it occurs, falls within the scope of this section on special road sections. At sign B6 on the approach road, driving behaviour will be tailored even more explicitly to that situation.


#### Location on the road/scene of the incident


It is important to note that the position to be taken on the carriageway must be adapted to the prevailing traffic conditions, unless the carriageway consists of lanes marked with arrows. This ensures the best possible flow of traffic and guarantees optimal use of the roundabout’s capacity. This aspect is the most important one regarding that position.


### 3.7 Special manoeuvres


The following are essential:
− the interests of other road users
− operation/control
− viewing behaviour
− let go first
The assessment criterion ‘Location of the incident’ is not essential. However, it may become essential if the choice of location gives rise to, or could give rise to, an unsafe situation. Assessing the various possibilities is a key element in this regard. Choosing a location that causes unnecessary risk, nuisance or environmental impact must be avoided.
If the departure following the manoeuvre is carried out differently and is related to the manoeuvre, the departure will be assessed under the ‘special manoeuvres’ section. In other cases, the departure falls under the ‘departure’ section.
During the test, in addition to getting in and out of the vehicle and the preparatory and checking procedures, two special manoeuvres are assessed. At least one of these manoeuvres must involve reversing.


#### Getting on and off


When assessing getting in and out of a vehicle, getting out is particularly important. Before opening the door, it is essential to look in the correct direction and give way to other road users.


#### Preparatory and control procedures


The assessment of preparatory and control procedures is merely supplementary; it is never decisive. For this reason, candidates are not asked any in-depth or difficult-to-answer questions on this subject.
The aim of these exercises is to increase the candidate’s independence in relation to the vehicle they are using to take part in traffic. This involves having some familiarity with the location and purpose of a number of key vehicle components. The candidate must also be aware of the purpose of certain checks.
The above serves as the basis for the questions put to the candidate during the preparation and assessment stages. No elements other than those included in the Driving Test Procedure are taken into account during the assessment.
Tyres The examiner will not ask any questions about the composition of tyres or the materials used in their manufacture, etc. However, you may be asked about the tread depth, the difference between summer and winter tyres, the risk of tyre damage, or, for example, why there is a cap on the valve.
Windows and mirrors Do not ask questions about the composition or construction of glass and the like. Do, however, ask, for example, about any potential danger or risk in the event of breakage or other damage. A question may also address the undesirability of objects that obstruct the view.
Oil level Do not ask any questions about types of oil, their composition, etc. Nor should the candidate be asked to check the oil level. However, the candidate must be able to identify the means by which the oil level can be checked and explain how the oil level can be checked.
Brake fluid Do not ask any questions about the purpose and function of brake fluid or its composition. However, the candidate must be able to identify the reservoir and explain how to check the fluid level. Also, regarding the possible consequences of the fluid level being too low
The level may be requested.
Coolant and windscreen washer fluid: Same as for brake fluid.
Battery It is sufficient for the candidate to be able to point out where it is located.
Driver assistance systems The examiner will not ask any questions about the technical, mechanical or software-related operation of driver assistance systems. An examiner will not, of their own accord, ask about systems that intervene in emergency situations, such as ESP, ABS and the emergency braking system. However, they may ask about any impact on the operation of driver assistance systems caused by damaged or dirty cameras and sensors.
Checks to be carried out in the car: The candidate must be able to identify what the various lights and gauges indicate. Instruments relating to safety, including driver assistance systems, are of particular importance. All lighting and other auxiliary equipment, including driver assistance systems, must be operated correctly. The candidate may be asked to adjust headrests to the most appropriate height and mirrors to the best possible position.


#### Reversing in a straight line


When reversing in a straight line, the candidate keeps the car in a virtually straight line on the right-hand side of the carriageway.
The manoeuvre is considered abnormal if the deviation is too great when maintaining a straight line.


#### Reversing in a curve


Whilst reversing in a curve, the candidate must maintain approximately the same distance from the right-hand side of the carriageway. Ending up on the opposite side of the carriageway to where the special manoeuvre began must be regarded as a deviation.


#### Parking in a perpendicular or diagonal bay


Although there is no explicit reference to a legal obligation when parking in a parking bay, it should be regarded as standard practice to give way to other traffic whilst carrying out this manoeuvre. The manoeuvre is deemed to be incorrect if, upon completion, the car comes to a halt partly within the adjacent parking space. The candidate is permitted to correct their manoeuvre if they are unable to park the car directly within the chosen space.


#### Parallel parking


Although no explicit legal obligation is referred to in relation to parking, it should be regarded as standard practice to give way to other traffic whilst carrying out this manoeuvre. The manoeuvre is deemed to be incorrect if the car is not parked immediately behind or as close as possible in front of the other vehicle, or if it is not sufficiently close to the side of the carriageway. The candidate is permitted to correct their manoeuvre if they are unable to park the car directly in the chosen space.


#### Turning around by crossing


The vehicle may touch the kerb at low speed. If there is a risk of this happening at too high a speed, react in good time to prevent damage. The manoeuvre is deemed to be incorrect if the candidate fails to turn round in the prescribed manner on a sufficiently wide road.


#### Turning around by a half turn


When turning by making a half-turn, the manoeuvre must be carried out without causing any danger or obstruction. This means that any approaching traffic must be at a safe distance.
The manoeuvre is deemed to be incorrect if the vehicle is not turned within the available space in a single movement, or if the turn is not performed smoothly enough or is carried out in a jerky manner.


#### Hill start


If the hill start manoeuvre is performed satisfactorily on the test course, it may be included in the assessment as one of the two compulsory special manoeuvres. In that case, the candidate need only perform one other special manoeuvre. When carrying out the hill start, it is up to the candidate to decide whether to use the handbrake, footbrake or a hill-start assist system.
